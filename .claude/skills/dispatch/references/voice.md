# The house voice

Malcolm Gladwell, applied to economics. The point is not imitation. It is that
a reader with no training should be able to follow an argument about the term
structure of interest rates without being condescended to.

## The measured target

`check.py` enforces these. They come from the strongest pieces on the site,
not from the corpus average, because several older dispatches drifted.

| | Target | The best pieces |
|---|---|---|
| Words per sentence | ≤ 12 | 10 to 11 |
| Sentences ≤ 8 words | ≥ 35% | 41 to 48% |
| Sentences ≥ 25 words | ≤ 4% | 0 to 3% |

**The distribution carries the voice, not the average.** A piece can average
12 words and still read wrong if it gets there by mixing 30-word sentences
with 4-word ones at the wrong moments. What makes it work is that most
sentences are short and almost none are long.

Reference points: `dispatch-ceiling-that-isnt` at 10.1 / 48% / 2%,
`dispatch-toilet-paper-theory` at 11.1 / 39% / 3%. Both read as the target.

## Hard rules

- **No em dashes.** Anywhere. Use a period or a comma.
- **US spelling.** Center, labor, color, behavior, neighborhood, defense.
- No exclamation marks, no emoji, no rhetorical throat-clearing.
- Never invent a number. Every figure traces to a source named in the piece.

## What actually makes it sound right

**Open concrete and stay there.** The instinct is to name the mechanism in
sentence two. Resist it for four or five sentences. Let the reader arrive at
the abstraction rather than being handed it.

Before:
> Nobody buys twice as much toilet paper because they got a raise. That
> sentence is the whole theory, and it has an awkward consequence that can be
> checked against quarterly filings.

After:
> Nobody buys twice as much toilet paper because they got a raise. Nobody buys
> half as much because they lost a shift. The package sits in a closet. You use
> what you use.
>
> That is the whole theory.

Same claim. The second earns it.

**Break a long sentence into its actual steps** rather than compressing.

Before (37w):
> What the theory did not anticipate at all is the shrinkflation channel, which
> is a third way the ceiling gets paid and the only one that is invisible in
> both the price line and the volume line.

After:
> What it did not anticipate is shrinkflation. That is a third way the ceiling
> gets paid. It is also the only one invisible in both the price line and the
> volume line.

**Use the short sentence as punctuation.** A three-word line after a long
paragraph does real work: *"Shrink the unit."* / *"It mattered."* /
*"They moved."*

**Second person, sparingly.** "You use what you use." It pulls the reader into
the example, and it is the closest available substitute for the people the
site does not have.

**Let the reversal carry the structure.** Set up a claim, let the evidence
complicate it, then resolve. The Volume Tell does this explicitly: the
category data confirms the prediction, the company filings appear to refute
it, and the resolution is the finding. Say the awkward part out loud rather
than omitting it.

## The known gap

There are almost no people in this corpus. Proper nouns are overwhelmingly
institutions: Kimberly-Clark, Dollar General, the CBO. Gladwell reports; this
site computes.

That cannot be fixed at the keyboard, so do not fake it. Never invent a person,
a quote, or a scene. Where a piece would be stronger with someone in it, second
person and a concrete object are the honest substitutes.

## Titles

Plain English, two to four words, and a small paradox where one is available.
*The Volume Tell*. *The Long End Votes*. *The Ceiling That Isn't*.

Not the coinage. "Scarcinality" is the reveal, not the headline.
