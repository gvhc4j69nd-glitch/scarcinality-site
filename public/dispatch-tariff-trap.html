The Tariff Trap

Every counterfeit scarcity this framework has examined arrived by accident, as the wreckage of a credit event that nobody designed. A tariff is the first one built on purpose. The goods exist. The factories abroad are running and the ships can sail. What has been inserted is a monetary wedge at the border, and the result is the treatise’s signature condition reached deliberately: full shelves and people going without.

A themed reading against the treatise and the first sustained application of the Toilet Paper Theory to a live policy. The claim is that the standard incidence tables measure the wrong quantity, that the tariff destroys the demand it is meant to redirect, and that its own legal clock is shorter than the capacity it claims to be building. Conditional, falsifiable, not policy advice.

Start with the receipt, because it is not in dispute. Through the first ten months of fiscal 2026 the United States collected $154.5 billion in customs duties. In the same ten months of fiscal 2024 it collected $62.7 billion. That is an increase of roughly $92 billion, or 146 percent, and it appears in the Treasury’s own accounts on the receipts side of the ledger, in the column with the income taxes and the payroll taxes, because that is what it is. The average effective tariff rate now stands at 11.8 percent on a pre-substitution basis, the highest since the early 1940s.

A tariff is a tax. The argument about whether foreign exporters or domestic buyers ultimately bear it is a real argument with real evidence on both sides, and the honest answer is that it depends on the elasticity of each traded good. But the collection point is not in question, the revenue is not in question, and the Budget Lab’s estimate is that the adjustment comes through prices rather than through nominal incomes, which means the price level rises by something between 0.5 and 0.7 percent and the average household loses somewhere between $760 and $940 a year.

That is where most analysis stops, and it is where this one starts, because the average household is precisely the object the Toilet Paper Theory says will mislead you.

The incidence table measures the wrong thing

Take the distributional estimates as published. In 2025 dollars, the annual cost to a household in the bottom decile is about $517. To a household in the top decile it is about $2,175. Read naively, that says the rich pay four times more, which is the reading that gets quoted when a tariff is defended.

Express it as a share of post-tax-and-transfer income and it inverts: 1.1 percent at the bottom against 0.4 percent at the top, a burden three times heavier on the poorest tenth. This is the standard regressivity finding, it is correct, and it is the one economists reach for.

The Toilet Paper Theory says both numbers understate what is happening, and it says so for a reason that is structural rather than rhetorical. A tariff is a tax on goods bought. The top decile pays its $2,175 and changes nothing about what it consumes, because the tax lands on a household whose necessity purchases are already at the satiation ceiling and whose income has room above them. It comes out of saving. The consumption is unaffected, the burden is real, and the dollars are all recorded.

The bottom decile cannot do this. Its income has no room above its necessities. So a portion of the tariff is never paid in dollars at all. It is paid by not buying the thing.

A household that stops buying the good pays no tariff on it, and therefore appears in the incidence table as less burdened. The instrument records the tax on the transactions that happened. The harm is in the transactions that did not.

This is the mechanism that makes the tariff regressive in a way the regressivity statistic cannot capture. The published burden at the bottom is bounded above by what the household could afford to spend. It is a measurement of expenditure, and expenditure at the bottom of the distribution is a measurement of constraint. The tables are not wrong. They are answering a different question from the one that matters, and the difference between the two questions is the entire subject of the Toilet Paper Theory.

Why this is a counterfeit, and a deliberate one

The treatise’s central test asks whether the thing has run out. Apply it here and the answer is unusually clean, because the tariff is the rare case where the answer is a matter of public record rather than inference.

Nothing has run out. The apparel that costs more is being manufactured at the same rate in the same factories. The ships still sail and the ports still work. No drought, no strike, no closed strait, no depleted seam. What changed is a schedule of rates, which is to say that a monetary wedge was inserted between a buyer who wants a good and a seller who has it. The good is not scarcer. The claim on it is.

That is the definition this framework uses for a counterfeit scarcity, and it is worth pausing on how unusual this instance is. Every other counterfeit the treatise examines is an accident: a dash for cash, a credit contraction, a lender withdrawing at the wrong moment. Nobody designs those. This one was designed, debated, and enacted, and the resulting condition is exactly what Section IV describes as the pathology to be avoided.

Real scarcity:    the thing is gone. Money cannot summon it.
Counterfeit:      the thing is here. The claim on it was withdrawn.
A tariff:         the thing is here. The claim on it was taxed.

Same condition at the shelf. Same household going without.
The only difference is that this one was on purpose.

This matters for the framework rather than only for the politics. Section VII says a counterfeit scarcity left standing stops being counterfeit and destroys the capacity it was pretending to threaten. A designed counterfeit runs the same clock as an accidental one. It does not get a dispensation for having been intended.

The trap

Now put the two halves together, because the interaction is what earns the title.

The case for a tariff is not that it raises revenue. It is that it redirects demand to domestic producers and thereby calls domestic capacity into being. Accept that on its own terms and ask what it requires. It requires that the demand which stops going abroad shows up at a domestic factory instead.

The Toilet Paper Theory says a meaningful part of that demand does not show up anywhere. The household at the bottom of the distribution does not switch to the domestic version at the higher price. It buys less, or it buys nothing. So the tariff redirects some demand, destroys some demand, and the destroyed portion is concentrated exactly in the volume segment that a new domestic entrant would need in order to justify building a plant.

The policy suppresses the demand that would justify the investment it is trying to induce. That is the trap, and it does not require anybody to have acted in bad faith. It is arithmetic.

The second jaw is time, and the previous dispatch measured it. Domestic capacity answers on a timescale of years: a TNT plant to 2029, submarines four years behind their contracts, a master welder who is thirty years of accumulated hours. Set that against the legal instrument currently carrying much of this tariff regime. After the Supreme Court held that the President lacked authority to impose the reciprocal tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the replacement was Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which permits duties without an investigation but is capped at 15 percent and limited to 150 days.

No one builds a factory against a 150-day price signal. The tariff imposes its cost on a timescale of days and asks for a response on a timescale of years, and the authority under which it is imposed expires long before the first slab of the plant it is meant to summon has cured. The Budget Lab’s baseline expects the effective rate to fall to 9.7 percent when Section 122 lapses, which tells you how much of the current wedge is standing on a clock.

The third jaw, which closes on the government

There is one more turn, and it is fiscal.

Customs duties are now running at $154.5 billion over ten months in a budget with a $1.799 trillion deficit over the same period. That revenue has become load-bearing. A tax that was introduced as leverage in a negotiation is now a line the Treasury is counting on, which is the ordinary way temporary taxes become permanent ones.

So the government arrives at a position where the tariff is hard to remove because the revenue is needed, and the revenue is needed partly because the tariff suppressed the activity that would otherwise have produced revenue through the income and payroll lines. A regressive consumption tax that has become fiscally necessary is the most durable kind, and it is durable in inverse proportion to who can afford it.

The instrument problem, for the fifth time

This framework has now found the same defect five times in three months, and at this point it is the finding rather than a curiosity attached to other findings. The Credit Counterfeit Index reads loose while subprime posts a thirty-two year record. Corporate profits and small-firm conditions diverge with no aggregate reconciling them. The federal survey of whether Americans go without is being discontinued. Unemployment and average hours cannot register a person working two jobs to cover one rent. And here, the standard tariff incidence table measures dollars paid, which is a measurement of what a household could still afford, and therefore reports the smallest burden for the households that were priced out entirely.

The pattern is consistent enough to state as a rule. Every instrument built to summarise an economy is built out of transactions, and a person who cannot transact does not appear in it. That is not a flaw in any particular index. It is what an average of dollars is.

Two caveats. The first is that tariff incidence is genuinely contested: the share ultimately borne by foreign exporters rather than domestic buyers varies by good and by elasticity, and estimates that assume near-complete pass-through will overstate the domestic burden for some categories. This dispatch relies on the Budget Lab’s assumption that the adjustment comes through prices, which is a defensible reading of the current evidence and is still a reading. The second is that a tariff can be the right instrument for reasons the framework does not price. Strategic dependence on an adversary for a critical input is a real problem, and the argument here is about the mechanism and the incidence, not about whether any particular protection is warranted.

What the framework does say is that the case for a tariff and the effect of a tariff are separated by the distribution. The case is made in aggregates: a trade balance, a domestic industry, a negotiating position. The effect lands on households that were already at their ceiling, and it lands as volume rather than as price, which is the one form the aggregates cannot see. The shelves stay full. The ships keep sailing. The factories abroad keep running. And somebody buys less of something ordinary, on purpose, by design, and does not appear anywhere in the accounts as having done so.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
