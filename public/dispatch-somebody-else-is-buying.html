Somebody Else Is Buying

A scientific workforce has three taps feeding it: the students who arrive, the money that keeps them working, and the institutions that hold the experienced ones. All three were turned down inside eighteen months. The interesting question is not what was lost, but where it went.

An extension of The Scarcity You Can’t Borrow Back, The Seed Corn Problem and Vitruvius Wrote It Down. Four claims: American science was never domestically self-sufficient at the graduate level, the channels compound rather than add, an appropriation that is passed is not the same as money that flows, and the destination data forces an amendment to something this site has argued twice. Conditional and falsifiable.

A country does not have scientists the way it has oil. It has them the way it has a crop: planted years before, tended continuously, and harvested by somebody.

Three things feed that crop. Students arriving, money sustaining the work, and institutions holding the people who already know how.

Between early 2025 and now, all three were restricted. Not sequentially. At once.

The first tap: who arrives

Foreign enrollment in American universities is projected down 9.5 percent this autumn. That is roughly 111,000 students who are not here.

The headline number understates the part that matters. Undergraduates pay tuition. Graduate students do research.

New foreign graduate enrollment fell an average of 24 percent across the institutions surveyed. Nearly two thirds of them expect another decline next year. Some are projecting drops as deep as 40 percent.

The mechanism is not mysterious. An executive order of 16 December 2025 restricted entry from 39 countries. There was no exception for student or exchange visitor visas. Appointment backlogs did the rest.

Spring enrollment this year was already down 20 percent against the spring before.

There was never a domestic pipeline

Here is the fact that decides how serious the first tap is. It is missing from most coverage of this.

American science was never domestically self-sufficient at the graduate level. It has not been for decades. It was not designed to be.

About 46 percent of the STEM doctorates awarded by American institutions go to students on temporary visas. Among STEM postdocs the share was above 57 percent. In chemistry it reached 64.

Now look at the workforce those pipelines produced. Foreign-born researchers are 43 percent of American scientists and engineers holding doctorates. Among doctorate-level computer and mathematical scientists, 58 percent were born abroad.

So a 24 percent cut to new foreign graduate entrants is not a trim to a supplement. It is a cut to the majority input. Of the whole system.

A country that loses scientists normally rebuilds by training more of its own. The United States cannot do that. Its training system was built on the import it has just restricted. At this scale there was never a domestic base to fall back on.

That is what turns a loss into a ratchet. The stock is leaving. The machinery that would replace it is being shut at the same time. And the inflow that fed the machinery is closed.

Three cuts that each look survivable, arranged so that none can be recovered from by doing more of the others.

The second tap: whether the work gets funded

This one has a twist worth slowing down for, because it is not the story people think it is.

The administration's 2026 budget proposed $44 billion in cuts across the research agencies, about 22 percent. The National Science Foundation was to lose 57 percent, the National Institutes of Health 41.

Congress rejected it. In January it passed an appropriation that increased NIH funding.

And the money still did not flow as appropriated. Disbursement slowed at NIH, NSF and NASA in the weeks after. In April the NSF closed a social science directorate, complying with a budget proposal that had been voted down.

An appropriation is a permission, not a payment. Congress can win the vote and lose the outcome, and this year it did both at once.

The effect shows up in the only number that matters to a laboratory. NIH awarded 5,564 fewer grants in fiscal 2025 than in fiscal 2024, a drop of 8.6 percent.

The 2027 request asks again: NIH down 13 percent, NSF down 55.

The third tap: who stays

The CDC is the clearest case because the personnel records are public.

In January 2025 it employed 12,934 federal staff. By March 2026 it employed 9,453.

That is 3,481 people, a fall of nearly 27 percent in fourteen months. Measured from September 2024 to May 2026 the reduction reaches 28.1 percent.

Composition is the part that should worry anyone. About half of those who left were scientists. Among the probationary employees terminated, 60 percent held a master's degree or a doctorate.

This was not a trimming of administrative overhead. It fell hardest on the people with the longest training behind them.

And it has not finished. A survey of current staff between February and April found 85 percent reporting burnout, and one in five saying they had decided to leave.

Why these do not simply add up

A reasonable person looks at that chart and totals it. That is the wrong arithmetic.

The three channels are the same pipeline at three different points. A pipeline fails at its narrowest section, not at its average.

Consider the sequence. A principal investigator loses a grant renewal. She cannot fund the postdoc position. The postdoc would have been filled by a foreign graduate student who could not get a visa in any case. The senior colleague who might have absorbed the project has taken early retirement from a federal agency.

Each of those is a modest percentage. Together they are a project that does not happen. And a person who does not become a scientist.

This is the Vitruvius problem again, and it is worse than the funding numbers suggest. The papers all survive. What stops is the production of people who can read them and do the next thing.

The turn

Now the part that changes the argument.

This site has twice written that scientific capacity, once dispersed, cannot be bought back. That capability lives in people and institutions rather than in documents, and that restoring a budget does not restore a network.

That is still true. It is also incomplete. The destination data is what completes it.

A Nature poll found 75 percent of researchers working in the United States considering leaving. Among early-career scientists it is closer to 80.

They are not merely considering. Applications to positions abroad rose 32 percent in the first quarter. That is against the same period two years earlier.

And the European Research Council has the cleanest count of all.

Applications from the United States for the ERC's senior researcher grants went from 23 to 114 in one year. Its early-career starting grant drew 169 American applications against 116 the year before.

Meanwhile China launched a K visa in October. It is aimed specifically at international science and technology graduates. More than a dozen established scientists moved from American universities to Chinese institutions over the summer.

So the amendment is this. Capability removed from a country is not destroyed. It is released onto a market, and the question is only whether anyone is bidding. Right now two large buyers are, and both are advertising.

Which makes this a different kind of loss

The distinction matters because it changes what the damage is and how long it lasts.

The scarcinality reading

The framework asks whether a shortage is real or manufactured.

A shortage of American scientists would be as manufactured as any on this site. Nobody has run out of people willing to do science. Not remotely. The applicants exist, the laboratories exist, the questions exist.

What was withdrawn was money and permission. Both are decisions.

But the framework has a rule about counterfeit shortages left standing too long. The treatise sets it out and the CDC piece tested it. A monetary scarcity, if it persists, converts into a real one. The money comes back and the network does not.

This dispatch adds the mechanism for the conversion. It is not decay. It is acquisition, and it runs on someone else’s schedule rather than on the budget cycle.

And the conversion is faster than the framework assumed, for the reason in the chart above. A system that imports half its doctoral candidates cannot wait out a bad decade. It has no bench. The moment the import stops, the domestic substitute that was never built is the thing you need immediately.

A researcher who takes a European grant signs for five years. A graduate student who enrolls in Toronto instead of Boston graduates there, and is hired there, and supervises students there. By the time an appropriation is restored, the thing it would have bought has a mortgage somewhere else.

What would prove this wrong

Three tests, all checkable within three years.

If foreign graduate enrollment recovers to trend once visa processing normalizes, the inflow damage was administrative rather than structural, and this piece overstates it.

The ERC surge may not convert into actual relocations. That would make 75 percent considering departure a sentiment rather than a behavior, and the transfer argument fails at its most important joint.

And CDC headcount may return toward 12,000 within two budget cycles, with comparable credentials. That would make federal scientific capacity more replaceable than this site has twice claimed. The borrow-back argument would need retiring rather than amending.

Cautions. Enrollment figures are institutional survey projections rather than completed counts, and the final tallies have historically come in above the spring estimates. The CDC headcount is solid because it is personnel data, but attributing every departure to policy would be wrong, since normal attrition continues. Grant counts are affected by a shift to forward funding as well as by budget pressure, which this piece has not separated. And the ERC numbers are applications, not moves. The surge is real and large; the relocations it produces will not be countable for another two years.
