The Same Move Twice

Take a package. Top income rate to 40 percent, corporate to 30, one-for-one breaks for money spent on plant and on people, and Medicare for All on top. It looks like two policies. It is one move made in two places, and it only works in one of them. The useful part is that you can tell which before anybody votes.

An applied reading against the treatise, The Counterfeit Shortage of Care and The Hours Ceiling. Three claims: both halves of this package reroute money rather than create resources, rerouting works on health care and fails on capacity, and the framework separates them in advance. Conditional, falsifiable, not policy advice and not investment advice.

Every one of these pieces has the same shape. Money that exists already is made to go somewhere else.

Raise the top rate and money moves from high earners to the Treasury. Raise the corporate rate and it moves from shareholders. Give a break for plant and payroll and it moves back, toward firms that build and hire. Put everyone on Medicare and it moves from employers and households into a single pool.

Nothing in that list creates a doctor, a turbine, or an hour of anybody's week.

That is not a criticism. Rerouting is a real and powerful instrument. But it has a precondition, and this framework exists to state it.

Moving money works when the thing you want already exists and people simply cannot reach it. It does nothing when the thing itself has run out. Every policy argument here turns on which situation you are in. And the two look identical from the outside.

First, the arithmetic

Start with what the tax side actually raises. The number is smaller than the debate suggests.

The top individual rate is 37 percent today, made permanent last year. Taking it to 40 is a three point rise. Comparable estimates put that near $600 billion over ten years. It depends heavily on where the bracket starts.

Corporate is 21 percent. Taking it to 30 is nine points. At roughly $130 to $140 billion a point, that is about $1.2 trillion over ten years. The true figure lands lower, because avoidance rises with the rate.

Call the package $1.75 trillion over a decade. About $175 billion a year.

That is worth absorbing before going further. The tax rises are not the funding mechanism for anything large. They are a change in who holds the money.

The half that works

Now health care, where this framework has already done the work.

America will spend about $5.9 trillion on health care this year. That is 18.6 percent of everything the country produces. The money is already being spent.

And as the earlier dispatch argued, nothing has run out. The hospitals are standing. The doctors are trained. The drugs are made. What is short is the ability to reach any of it. That is a monetary problem wearing the costume of a physical one.

Which is exactly the condition where rerouting works.

The seven major cost studies average $46 trillion over ten years for single payer. That number gets quoted as though it were new spending. It is not. Set against what the country already spends, the same average shows a net decrease in national health expenditure of about 5.7 percent.

The bill does not grow. The envelope changes.

What rises is the federal share. It rises enormously, by something like 221 percent in one estimate. But that is a transfer of an existing bill from employers and households onto the government, not an addition to what the country spends.

So the honest verdict on this half is favorable. It has nothing to do with politics. Care is a counterfeit shortage. A counterfeit shortage is cured by restoring reach. Rerouting restores reach.

The financing model on this site puts the same point in one line. The money does not appear. It changes address.

The half that does not

Now the capital and labor breaks, where the same instrument meets a different kind of wall.

Two things have to be said plainly before the argument.

The first is that the capital half already exists. Full expensing for equipment was made permanent last year, along with immediate expensing for domestic research. A dollar of plant is already a dollar deducted, at once. There is no further one-for-one to give.

The second is that labor has always had it. Wages are deducted in the year they are paid and always have been. To give labor something beyond that means a credit worth more than the wage itself, which is a different and far more expensive instrument than the phrase suggests.

But set the mechanics aside, because the deeper problem is not mechanical.

The deeper problem is that the constraint on American capacity right now is not willingness. It is physical.

That last row is the one to hold onto. It is not a prediction. It already happened. Congress voted the money and the shells did not appear.

The labor row deserves more care. It is the most interesting and the most arguable.

Subsidize the demand for labor when the supply of hours is fixed and you do not get more work. You get a higher price for the same work. With consumer prices at 3.4 percent and the Federal Reserve raising rates into it this month, adding wage pressure is poorly timed.

The honest counter is slack. Unemployment sits at 4.1 percent, near a four year high. Some of a labor subsidy would pull people into jobs rather than bidding up the people already in them. How much depends on participation. That is the number to watch, not the wage.

What 30 percent specifically does

The corporate number was pinned. It deserves a straight answer, not a shrug.

The United States levies 21 percent federally, about 25.6 percent once state taxes are counted. The average across OECD countries is 21.2 percent combined.

Take the federal rate to 30 and the American combined rate lands near 34.6 percent. That is roughly 13 points above the international average. Counting states, it is higher than the 35 percent federal rate that stood before 2018.

Two consequences follow and both are real.

Reported profit moves. Not factories, necessarily, but the paperwork that assigns income to jurisdictions. This is why the Congressional Budget Office scores corporate increases below their mechanical value. Read the $1.2 trillion above as a ceiling.

And a gap opens. A 40 percent top personal rate against a 30 percent corporate rate is ten points of daylight. Every point of it rewards turning personal income into business income. Effort flows into recharacterization, which produces accountants rather than output.

The version that would work

The framework does not say do nothing. It says aim.

Do the health care rerouting. The constraint there is monetary. The resources are standing. Moving money is the instrument built for exactly that.

Do not spend the capital and labor breaks generally. A broad subsidy lands mostly where capital was not the bottleneck. And a subsidy paid into a queue produces a longer queue at a higher price.

Spend it narrowly instead, on the things that have actually run out. Electrician apprenticeships. Transformer manufacturing. Machine tooling for munitions. Grid interconnection staff.

That is a smaller policy and a much duller one. It is also the one that ends with more goods in the world rather than the same goods at a higher price.

The order of operations dispatch made the sequencing rule, and it applies cleanly here. Dissolve the fake shortages first, and never let the repair cross onto ground where the shortage is real.

What would prove this wrong

Three things, and the first is the sharpest.

If a broad capital subsidy is enacted and the interconnect queue shortens, the physical reading is wrong and the constraint was financial after all. Watch project counts, not dollars committed.

Say a labor credit is enacted and hours per worker rise above 35, with wage growth staying moderate. Then the hours ceiling is softer than twenty years of data suggest. The labor half would be better than argued here.

And say a single payer transition raises total health spending rather than lowering it. Then the counterfeit reading of care is wrong. What looked like a distribution problem was a real one.

Three cautions. The revenue figures are scaled from published estimates of smaller rate changes. They are not a score of this package, which nobody has written. The single payer studies disagree sharply with one another. The average quoted here hides a spread wide enough to contain both savings and losses. And this dispatch takes no position on whether any of it should be done. That is a question about what a country owes its members, not about which shortage is real.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
