Dark Fiber With a Power Bill

Roughly $630 billion of capital spending is going into data centres this year, against a scarcity that money cannot relieve on the timescale the money is being spent. The interesting question is not whether it overbuilds. The chief executive of Microsoft has already said it will. The question is what an overbuilt data centre actually is once the financing stops, and the answer is not the one the fibre-optic comparison implies.

A themed reading against the treatise, and the third consecutive case in which the framework has found a genuine rather than a counterfeit shortage. The others were the Hormuz closure and the munitions build-out. Three in a row is either a change in conditions or a change in the reader, and the closing section takes that seriously. Conditional, falsifiable, not investment advice.

Apply the framework’s only test and the answer arrives immediately. Does the constraint answer a bid? Bid for graphics processors and they arrive, in quantity, on a schedule. Bid for capital and it arrives in almost unlimited size: roughly $630 billion of hyperscaler capital spending this year against $388 billion last year, a rise of 62 percent, with an estimated $4.1 trillion of associated debt financing standing behind the wider build-out. Bid for electrical power and nothing happens at all.

Power is now the stated primary constraint on new construction, ahead of chips and far ahead of money. At the end of 2025 roughly 8,200 projects representing about 1,312 gigawatts of generation were sitting in United States interconnection queues, and in some regions a project relying solely on the grid faces up to ten years to energisation. That is the shape of a real scarcity: an unlimited bid meeting a bounded thing and producing a queue rather than a delivery.

What is actually scarce

Underneath the power constraint sit four physical bottlenecks, none of which is monetary and none of which shortens because more money arrives.

Large power transformers     24 to 36 months, up to 4 years for high voltage
Standard power transformers  128-week average lead time
Generator step-up units      144-week average lead time
Heavy-duty gas turbines      GE Vernova about 55 GW in queue
                         Siemens Energy backlog €136 billion, its largest ever
Electricians                  a multi-year apprenticeship, against retirements

The last line is the one that ties this to everything else on this site. The president of Microsoft has named the shortage of electrical talent as the single largest problem slowing data centre construction in the United States. Not capital. Not permits. People who can pull conductor and terminate switchgear, whose number is set by a training pipeline measured in years, which is the hours ceiling arriving in the most capital-rich industry on earth. A trillion dollars does not produce a journeyman.

There is a further detail that ought to be uncomfortable for everyone involved. The transformer bottleneck runs on grain-oriented electrical steel, and China holds roughly 60 percent of global transformer manufacturing capacity. The build-out framed as a race against China depends on Chinese equipment to energise it, and the tariff regime taxes that equipment on the way in.

The scissors

Now put the cost of building beside the price of what the building produces, because this is where the sustainability question is actually decided.

An asset financed across five and a half years, built with equipment on multi-year lead times, is producing an output whose price has fallen ninety-five percent in two. That is not a demand problem. Demand for inference is enormous and growing. It is a price problem, and the two are easy to confuse because the volume charts look wonderful while the revenue per unit collapses underneath them.

The accounting has already begun absorbing the strain. Meta extended the assumed useful life of its servers and network assets from four years to five and a half, which reduced its 2025 depreciation charge by $2.9 billion, and every major hyperscaler made a similar extension between 2020 and 2025. The bear case, argued most loudly by Michael Burry, is that the real economic life of a high-end accelerator is closer to two or three years and that the extensions understate depreciation by something like $176 billion across 2026 to 2028. The framework has no view on whose estimate is right. It has a view on what the dispute is about, which is that the asset’s life is being estimated by the people who need it to be long.

China is not racing to win the model

The Chinese build-out is usually read as a capacity race, and on the surface it looks like one: a drafted plan worth about $295 billion over five years for a national computing grid, running on roughly 80 percent domestic silicon, structurally excluding Nvidia and AMD. National computing capacity was near 215 exaflops in 2025 and is projected above 330 by the end of 2026, growth of about 40 percent a year. Huawei shipped some 812,000 Ascend accelerators in 2025.

Read through this framework, that is the less important half. The capacity is the physical layer. What China is actually doing is attacking the monetary layer, and the treatise says the monetary layer is the one that comes first.

The mechanism is price. The performance gap between open and closed models narrowed from 8 percent to 1.7 percent in a single year. A Huawei-backed model now sells inference at eleven cents per million tokens. DeepSeek sits at twenty-seven cents, Google’s cheapest tier at twenty-five, and OpenAI has cut its own rates by as much as 80 percent in response. A state-directed competitor that does not require a return on its capital sets the price for every competitor that does.

China does not have to win the model race. It has to make winning it worthless, and giving away something excellent is the cheapest way to do that. You cannot service four trillion dollars of debt on an output that a rival is content to price at eleven cents.

This is the same manoeuvre the framework describes everywhere else, run in reverse and on purpose. The American build-out is an enormous bet on the physical layer financed by claims. The Chinese response does not contest the physical layer. It devalues the claims by collapsing the price of what the physical layer makes.

Will it oversaturate, and who survives

It will, and the people building it say so. Satya Nadella has stated plainly that there will be an overbuild and that compute prices will fall further when the correction arrives. Meanwhile every hyperscaler reports being supply-constrained rather than demand-constrained, and three of the four lost market value after their most recent earnings calls. Both things are true at once, which is the ordinary condition of the late phase of a capital cycle: physically short today, financially long tomorrow.

The framework’s answer to who survives is not the vague one about strong companies. It is specific, and it follows from the ordering. Whoever owns the bounded thing survives, and the bounded thing is not compute.

Abundant, and therefore not a moat:
  capital, accelerators, model weights, engineers, floor space

Bounded, and therefore the whole game:
  interconnection rights, firm power contracts, transformers already
  delivered, self-generation, and the crews to install it

A firm that financed with equity and holds signed power holds an option. A firm that financed with the circular arrangements now estimated at more than $800 billion, in which vendors fund their own customers, holds an obligation. When the price of the output falls to marginal cost, the first can wait and the second cannot, and that distinction has nothing to do with which of them has the better model.

What happens to the overcapacity

The fibre analogy is the one everybody reaches for and it is half right, which makes it worse than useless if the wrong half is taken.

The right half: at the end of the telecom boom the physical asset survived the financing entirely. Equity in the builders went to zero, the glass stayed in the ground, and the capacity was eventually bought for cents and lit by people who had not paid for it. The counterfeit was in the claims layer, and the claims layer is what got destroyed. The real layer was fine. That is the treatise’s distinction made concrete, and it is the most likely shape of an AI capacity correction too: a wealth transfer from the financiers of capacity to its second owners.

The wrong half, and the reason for the title: unlit fibre cost almost nothing to hold. It sat dark for a decade with no meter running, and a decade later it was worth a great deal because the glass had not aged. A data centre cannot do this. It carries a power contract whether or not the racks are earning, a cooling load, a maintenance bill, and silicon depreciating on a clock measured in a few years rather than in decades. Idle fibre waits. Idle compute rots.

So the surviving asset is not the computer. It is the hole in the ground with a grid connection attached, and in a market where energisation can take ten years, the connection is worth more than everything plugged into it.

That is the concrete prediction. In a correction, the accelerators are written down hard and fast, because their economic life is short and their replacement is better. The shell, the substation, the interconnect and the water rights are not written down at all in real terms, and may appreciate, because they are the scarce thing and cannot be reproduced faster by anyone with money. Expect distressed sales priced on megawatts rather than on racks.

Who pays for it in the meantime

The build-out is not being financed only by its investors, and this is the part that connects it to the Toilet Paper Theory rather than to the market pages.

In the PJM capacity market, prices went from $28.92 per megawatt-day for the 2024 to 2025 delivery year to $329.17 for 2026 to 2027. Data centres were responsible for about 63 percent of the increase in the 2025 to 2026 auction, some $9.3 billion. That flows into ordinary bills: about $21 a month more for Pepco residential customers, roughly $18 in western Maryland, about $16 in Ohio, and a projection of near $70 a month for an average family in the region by 2028.

Electricity is the clearest case of a necessity with no satiation ceiling in dollar terms. A household cannot decide to consume meaningfully less of it without giving up heat or cooling, so the price does not destroy volume the way a tariff on goods does. It rises to absorb whatever income exists. The bottom decile pays the capacity charge and does not hold the equity, and the transfer runs through a regulated tariff sheet that nobody voted on.

Three real shortages in a row, which is worth pausing on

This framework spent its first year finding counterfeits nearly everywhere it looked, and has now found three genuine scarcities consecutively: the Strait, the munitions, and the grid. Either conditions changed or the reader did, and intellectual honesty requires naming the second possibility rather than only the first.

The defence of the first reading is that all three cases share a feature the earlier ones did not. Each involves an unconstrained bid meeting a physical process with a long, irreducible cycle time: a shipping lane, an explosives plant, a transformer works. That is a specific and checkable condition, not a mood. But the framework should be uncomfortable, and the discomfort belongs in the ledger rather than in a footnote.

One more connection is worth stating because it constrains what this dispatch is allowed to claim. The gauge’s forward reading found that a serious data-centre credit unwind, taken alone, produces a Monetary Bind of about plus 0.90 against a threshold of plus 1.0. It falls short. An AI capital correction, on its own, is not a credit counterfeit by this framework’s own instrument. It needs a monetary companion, and nothing here should be read as predicting one.

Two caveats. Capital expenditure figures for 2026 are guidance rather than outturn and the published estimates range from about $630 billion for the largest four firms to roughly $750 billion for the fourteen largest operators, so the exact number should be treated as a band. And the claim that idle compute rots faster than idle fibre depends on the pace of accelerator obsolescence, which is the single most contested number in this whole subject and the one on which the depreciation argument turns; if high-end accelerators turn out to hold economic value for eight years rather than three, much of the urgency here dissolves.

What does not depend on that number is the ordering. The money is abundant, the power is not, and no quantity of the first produces the second inside the period the financing assumes. The build-out will get further than its critics expect, because a bid this large does move a great deal of physical world, and it will arrive later and cost more than its promoters expect, because the last mile of it runs through a transformer works and a training pipeline. When it corrects, the claims will be destroyed and the concrete will not, and the people who end up owning the concrete will mostly not be the people who paid for it. That is the oldest story in this framework. It is only unusual here because everyone can see it coming and is building anyway.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
