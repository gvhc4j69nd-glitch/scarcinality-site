Off the Books

The argument about taxes and public spending has run for seventy years on a question the American record cannot answer. Ask a narrower one instead. How much of corporate America’s productivity was paid for by somebody else, and what did corporate America pay toward it? Both numbers can be worked out, and they are not close.

Four claims: the US time series cannot separate tax rates from public investment because they moved together, the tax side fails its own tests while the public R&D side passes, the resulting benefit to business is large and computable, and the structure that keeps it unacknowledged is a free-rider problem rather than hypocrisy. Every calculation is shown so it can be checked. Conditional and falsifiable.

Here is the question as it is usually put. Did America grow because taxes were low enough to free private investment? Or because public money built the roads and the research the private economy ran on?

The American record cannot answer that. Not because the data is missing. Because of how it moved.

Read the second and fourth columns together. Taxes came down. Public research came down with them. Growth came down too.

That pattern fits three stories equally well. High taxes did no harm. Public investment did the work. Or neither mattered, and something else was going on.

Two variables that move together cannot be told apart by watching them move. Anyone citing the 1950s for either side of this argument is reading a series that cannot support the claim, in either direction.

The aggregate is a dead end. The two claims have to be tested separately, and when they are, they do not come out the same.

What survives testing

On the tax side, very little.

The correlation between the top marginal rate and GDP growth is about 0.03. That is not a weak relationship. It is the absence of one.

The Congressional Research Service looked back to 1945. It found no statistically significant relationship between top marginal rates, or top capital gains rates, and real per capita growth.

The 2017 cut is the cleanest modern test and the result is split. Firms with an average-sized tax shock raised domestic investment by about 20 percent. That is real. But the congressional review of the whole literature found no significant effect on the economy. And the sector pattern is perverse. The cost of capital for structures fell 11.7 percent and investment in structures grew 5. The after-tax cost of research rose 3.4 percent and investment in intellectual property grew 7.7.

On the public research side, the evidence is of a different quality altogether.

Fieldhouse and Mertens identify off appropriations shocks rather than correlations. They find social returns to public R&D of 140 to 210 percent. And they find that government-funded research accounts for roughly one fifth of all business-sector total factor productivity growth since the Second World War.

Aschauer’s older work on public capital points the same way. He put the elasticity of private output with respect to public capital at 0.24 to 0.36. That implies returns several times those on private capital. Later studies found smaller numbers and the same sign. The mechanism is not that public investment competes with private investment. It is that public capital raises the return on private capital.

Putting a number on it

That one fifth can be turned into something concrete, and this is the part worth doing slowly.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts private business productivity growth at 1.4 percent a year from 1948 to 1986. From 1987 to 2023 it was 0.8 percent. Compound those and productivity today sits at about 2.26 times its 1948 level.

Now strip out a fifth of that growth, which is the share attributed to federally funded research.

And what did business pay for it

Now the other side of the ledger. It is smaller than almost anyone would guess.

The enacted federal nondefense research budget for 2026 is $94.1 billion. Corporate income tax receipts for the same year were $381.4 billion, against total federal revenue near $5.2 trillion.

So the corporate sector supplies about 7.3 percent of federal revenue. Apply that share to the research budget.

Where the tax cuts went instead

For contrast, follow the money the other way.

The 2017 corporate tax savings across the S&P 500 were allocated like this. Thirty-nine percent to shareholders through buybacks and dividends. Twenty-three percent to business investment. Fifteen to wages, bonuses and benefits, and three to philanthropy. Large firms put under a fifth of their savings into capital spending and research combined.

The 280 largest corporations ran $2.7 trillion of buybacks between 2018 and 2022.

Now ask where a buyback lands. The wealthiest tenth of American households hold 88.1 percent of corporate equities. The top one percent hold about half. The bottom half of the country holds roughly one percent.

And nothing was built. The money changed hands and stopped. Compare that to a research budget whose output cannot be fenced, which is exactly why its social return runs above 100 percent.

The clearest single case

Pharmaceuticals make the capture visible in a way nothing else does.

NIH funding contributed to research behind every one of the 210 drugs approved between 2010 and 2016. For 2010 to 2019 the figure is 386 of 387. That is 99.7 percent.

The public bill was $247.3 billion, or about $711 million per approved drug. That is comparable to what the industry itself reports spending per approval. Of the public money, 83 percent went to basic research on the biological targets rather than to the drugs.

So the public funds the half of the work that cannot be patented. The half that can be patented is priced back to the same public. The march-in rights that exist in law to address this have never been used over price.

The public pays twice. Once at the National Institutes of Health, and again at the pharmacy counter. That is not an accident of the system. It is the design, and the design was chosen.

The objection, and the answer to it

There is an obvious rebuttal to all of this and it deserves a straight response.

If public investment is so good for business, why are corporate profits at records now, in the lowest public investment era since the war?

They are. Pre-tax profits reached $4.8 trillion, about 18 percent of national income. Profits as a share of GDP are 15.85 percent, against 8 percent in 1982. Margins hit 19.4 percent. That is a level unseen since the 1940s.

Here is the resolution, and it matters.

Profit share and productivity growth are different things. They have moved in opposite directions. Growth fell from 4.5 percent a year to 2.4. Over the same stretch the profit share roughly doubled. Labor’s share of output fell to 52.8 percent. That is the lowest since the series began in 1947.

That is a larger slice of a slower-growing pie. Extraction rather than creation, achieved through market power and a compressed wage bill rather than through a faster engine.

Record profits are not evidence that the engine is fine. They are consistent with an engine running slower and a smaller number of people standing closer to the exhaust.

Why it stays unacknowledged

Which brings the question to its real answer. If the benefit is this large, why does corporate America not say so?

Not hypocrisy. Arithmetic.

Look at the shape of it.

A benefit that is diffuse, delayed, shared with rivals and impossible to book loses every argument. The cost it loses to is concentrated, immediate and itemized. And it loses even when it is fifty times larger.

Every firm benefits. No firm needs it to post a good quarter. All of them lobby against the tax that funds it. Each of those three statements is individually rational, and together they describe a system that defunds its own engine.

The scarcinality reading

The framework asks which scarcity is binding. This is a case where the answer has been measured and then ignored.

A tax rate is a monetary variable. It moves claims between parties, and the evidence that it moves national growth is close to absent.

Public research builds capability. It is the slow kind of thing this site keeps describing. Expensive to make, impossible to buy quickly, and worth multiples of what it costs.

Those are different categories, which is the whole thesis, and the evidence here lines up with it exactly. The monetary lever shows weak aggregate effects. The capability lever shows strong ones. And the capability lever is the one being cut, by 41 percent in one proposal and 13 in the next.

The era American business holds up as its golden age of global dominance was the high-tax, high-public-investment era. Its firms outcompeted a rebuilding world on an infrastructure and research base the public had paid for. The measured return on that base was several times what private capital was earning.

What would prove this wrong

Three tests.

Independent work may overturn the Fieldhouse and Mertens attribution and put the public share of business TFP growth near zero. Then the central calculation collapses and this piece is wrong.

Business productivity growth may hold or accelerate through the next decade while federal research spending keeps falling as a share of GDP. That would break the link, and the lag argument with it.

And firms that lobbied hardest for the 2017 cut may show capital spending well above those that did not. That would make the investment channel stronger than the aggregate suggests, and the tax side deserves more credit than this piece gives it.

Cautions, and they are real. The 18 percent is an estimate built on an estimate. It takes one paper’s attribution share and carries it through BLS productivity rates to a level. The honest claim is the order of magnitude, not the number. The $6.9 billion assumes the corporate sector funds research in proportion to its share of federal revenue. That is a convention, not a fact. Corporations also pay payroll taxes and their owners pay individual tax, so a wider definition would raise it. Profit-share and productivity series are not directly comparable and are used here as separate observations rather than as a single ratio. And every attribution in this piece is correlational at the decade scale. The appropriations-shock identification is the strongest evidence here, and it is the reason the public-investment side is stated more firmly than the tax side.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
