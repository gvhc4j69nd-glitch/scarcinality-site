The Counterfeiter's Gauge

The framework asks which shortage is real. This dispatch turns the question into a number: the Credit Counterfeit Index, updated quarterly, with a threshold, twenty years of history, and a standing call it can be wrong about.

An instrument of the framework, built on Fisher, Keynes, and Minsky. The gauge below reads live from the site's data file and updates each quarter. Inputs are quarterly approximations from public sources; the Real Bind series is a published judgment. Conditional, falsifiable, not investment advice.

Every dispatch so far has answered the framework's question, which shortage is real, one case at a time. Health care: counterfeit. Hormuz: real. But a framework that only speaks in essays can dodge its own verdicts. An instrument cannot. So this dispatch builds one: a single quarterly number that says when credit has become the counterfeiter, meaning when the ordering of money is manufacturing scarcity that does not physically exist. It has a definition, a threshold, twenty years of history with two clean breaches separated by nearly fifteen years of clear readings, and a needle that points somewhere right now.

The definition

Counterfeiting is not tight money. Tight money during a genuine physical shortage is the system responding to reality, as it did during the supply-chain era and again during the oil shock. Counterfeiting is tight money while the shelves are full: a monetary bind pressing on an economy with idle capacity, freezing transactions that are physically possible. So the index is a difference of two binds.

CCI  =  MONETARY BIND  \u2212  REAL BIND

MONETARY BIND = mean z-score of four inputs:
  \u00b7 high-yield credit spread (the price of credit risk)
  \u00b7 net share of banks tightening business lending standards
  \u00b7 real policy rate (fed funds minus core inflation)
  \u00b7 real money growth, inverted (starvation when negative)

REAL BIND = intensity of genuine physical scarcity
  (supply-chain pressure, energy or transit disruption, true labor shortage)

TIPPING POINT:  CCI \u2265 +1.0 sustained for a quarter

The subtraction is the framework's signature move. The four monetary inputs are ordinary; what makes the gauge scarcinality's is that monetary tightness only counts as counterfeiting when there is no real bind to justify it. One full standard deviation of unjustified monetary restriction, held for a quarter, is the tipping point: the level at which the money ordering starts stopping trades the physical world would happily clear.

The gauge

What twenty years show

Running the gauge back to 2006 produces eighty-two quarters of readings, plus two projected. Four breach the tipping point: the final quarter of 2008, the first quarter of 2009, and the second and third quarters of 2023. Fifteen years separate the two episodes. In the span between them the index warned, retreated, and fell deeply negative, but never held above the line. The pattern is not noise.

The 2008 reading carries a subtlety that the short history missed. In the first three quarters of that year the monetary bind was already extreme: high-yield spreads blowing out, banks tightening at rates not seen since the early 1990s. But the index stayed clear, because oil was genuinely scarce. Crude reached a hundred and forty-five dollars in July 2008, supply-chain pressure was real, and the Real Bind offset the Monetary Bind almost point for point. The gauge held at warning, not breach. Then in the fourth quarter oil collapsed, the real shortage evaporated, and the monetary bind was left standing alone. CCI hit plus 1.42. The financial crisis reads, in the framework's terms, as a credit counterfeit that was temporarily masked by a genuine commodity shock, and the masking was part of what made the unmasking so violent.

The intervening years are equally instructive for what they do not show. The 2011 European debt crisis registered only as a warning in the United States: American monetary conditions stayed loose, M2 was growing near ten percent, and the real bind had faded. The 2015 and 2016 high-yield energy stress, when spreads touched eight percent and lending standards tightened, reached warning territory but not breach, because the stress was sector-specific and the broader money supply remained ample. The gauge did not cry wolf either time. And 2020 is the most striking negative: SLOOS tightened to seventy percent, the sharpest reading in the data, yet CCI fell to minus one and a half and kept falling. The M2 flood was overwhelming. The supply shortages were real. Whatever was scarce in 2020 and 2021 was emphatically not credit's doing, and the gauge said so.

Then the breach. In the second and third quarters of 2023 the index crossed and held above the tipping point, and the counterfeits arrived on schedule. Existing-home sales collapsed toward their lowest level since 1995 while the physical housing stock per person was unchanged: owners locked into three percent mortgages would not list against seven, so the inventory shortage was manufactured entirely by the money ordering. Three banks failed in a system whose total deposits were ample. Business lending contracted, small-firm borrowing costs pushed past nine percent, and factory surveys sat below the boom-bust line for sixteen straight months while the capacity stood idle. And the tell, the framework's favorite kind of evidence: these shortages began resolving as rates fell through 2024 and 2025, not through building anything. Bank funding stress eased and lending standards loosened first; the housing lock-in unwound more slowly, with sales still near their lows through 2024 before recovering. Nothing real was added at any point. The money was reordered, and the scarcity dissolved on the money's schedule, which is what counterfeit means.

Four breach quarters in eighty-two readings, falling in two episodes fifteen years apart. Both episodes coincide with textbook credit counterfeits. The fifteen years between them include two near-misses the gauge correctly declined to call. The discrimination holds.

Where the needle points now

The index reads minus 1.08 for the third quarter of 2026, observed through mid-August, against minus 1.36 in the second. That is a move of 0.28 toward the tipping point, and the projection for the fourth quarter, minus 0.92, continues in the same direction. The needle is rising.

It is rising for none of the reasons the July reading gave, which is the part worth setting out carefully, because a gauge that goes the right way for the wrong reason is telling you something different from what it appears to be telling you.

The July call had two conditions joined by an and: that the penciled hikes would land, and that the oil pipeline would clear. Only the second is happening, and it is happening slowly.

The hikes did not land at all. The effective federal funds rate sits at 3.63 percent against core PCE inflation of 3.3 percent, putting the real policy rate near plus 0.33 percent, essentially unchanged from the second quarter. M2 grew 5.3 percent over the year to June on the Federal Reserve’s own H.6, which is real growth near 2 percent. High-yield spreads closed at 271 basis points on 12 August, among the tightest readings in the series. The July Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey reported business lending standards basically unchanged for firms of all sizes, with banks charging narrower rate spreads to large and middle-market firms. Add those together and the Monetary Bind actually fell, from minus 0.06 to minus 0.08. Money did not tighten. It loosened slightly.

So the entire rise in the index comes from the other side of the subtraction. The Real Bind eased from 1.3 to 1.0 as the acute phase of the conflict ended in June, and subtracting a smaller number from an unchanged one produces a larger result. Brent still traded between roughly $88 and $91 through the first half of August with the Strait of Hormuz contested, and the Energy Information Administration expects Middle East production to return to near pre-conflict levels only in early 2027, with disruption near 0.6 million barrels a day persisting through next year. The real constraint is fading, not gone.

2026Q2   MB −0.06  −  RB 1.3  =  −1.36
2026Q3   MB −0.08  −  RB 1.0  =  −1.08

The index rose 0.28. The monetary side contributed −0.02 of that.
All of the movement, and slightly more, came from the war ending.

The needle is moving toward the line because a real shortage is receding, not because money is tightening. That is the gauge working exactly as defined, and it is also the reading most likely to be misquoted, so it is worth saying in one sentence: this is not an early warning.

The July dispatch projected minus 0.66 for this quarter and minus 0.01 for the fourth, which would have put the index halfway to a breach by the end of the year. The actual path runs roughly four tenths to nine tenths below that. The direction of the call is right so far and its mechanism is absent, which means the arrival time it implied is not supportable.

That distinction can be made precise, and it produces a better forward statement than the July one. Suppose the Strait reopens fully and the Real Bind goes to zero, with every monetary input left exactly where it is today. The index would sit at about minus 0.1, because that is what the Monetary Bind alone reads. A fading energy shock cannot carry the gauge to plus 1.0 no matter how completely it fades. Reaching the tipping point requires the Monetary Bind itself to rise by roughly a full standard deviation, which in the units of the four inputs means credit spreads at genuine stress levels, banks tightening at net shares in the tens rather than the low single digits, and a real policy rate several points higher than today. None of those is present, and none is close.

One input has been revised. The second quarter of 2026 previously carried a bank-tightening reading of 8, estimated before the survey covering that quarter was published. The July survey put standards basically unchanged, so the input is revised to 3 and the quarter reads minus 1.36 rather than minus 1.31. The revision is small and it moves in the same direction as everything else in this update: monetary conditions were looser than the gauge had assumed.

What the reevaluation found, which is a defect

The more important part of this update is not the number. It is that four other dispatches have now identified the same blind spot in this framework's instruments, and this gauge is the one that started the list. It read minus 1.31 while subprime auto delinquency was posting a thirty-two year record. An index that says credit is not binding, in a quarter when credit was plainly binding for the households at the bottom of the distribution, has a problem that a decimal place will not fix.

The July survey shows the mechanism in a single document, which is what makes it worth stating precisely rather than in general terms. In the same release, banks reported easing loan rate spreads for large and middle-market firms and a net share reported tightening standards on credit cards. Two opposite movements, in one survey, in one quarter. The number this index consumes from that survey is the business-lending line, which nets to basically unchanged. So the gauge records a quiet quarter, and the quiet is an average of an easing at the top and a tightening at the bottom.

What the gauge reads:  C&I standards, basically unchanged
What the same survey also says:
  · narrower rate spreads to large and middle-market firms
  · a net share of banks tightening credit card standards

The aggregate is not wrong. It is the mean of two
opposite signals, and the framework cares about both.

The household data says the same thing more slowly. The New York Fed's second-quarter report put total household debt at $18.8 trillion with 4.7 percent of balances delinquent, and the headline improved slightly. Underneath it, the share of auto loans transitioning into serious delinquency rose from 2.93 to 3.0 percent over the year, and mortgages entering serious delinquency rose from 1.29 to 1.52 percent, which is an increase of nearly a fifth in relative terms. The aggregate improved. Two of its components did not.

The fix, and why it is not shipped today

The obvious repair is a fifth input to the Monetary Bind capturing credit conditions at the bottom of the distribution: consumer standards rather than business standards, serious delinquency transitions in auto and card, and the rejection rate on credit applications. Call it the distributional companion.

It is not being added to the published series today, for a reason that should be stated rather than buried. The gauge's only real asset is a consistent history back to 2006 and two breach episodes it identified under a fixed definition. Adding an input now, with the benefit of knowing what the last two years did, would let this framework tune an instrument to fit a period it has already read, and the resulting track record would be worth nothing. The honest sequence is to define the companion, publish its definition before the next reading, and let it be scored forward from a date that is already on the record.

A gauge that is quietly improved every time it misses something is not an instrument. It is a narrator. The defect is published today and the repair is dated forward, which is the only version of this that can be checked later.

The standing call is therefore restated rather than reissued. The index will not approach the tipping point while the real policy rate sits near zero, credit spreads price no risk, and a genuine energy constraint persists. Conditions for a breach require the monetary bind to rise while the real bind falls, and as of August 2026 the real bind is falling slowly and the monetary bind is not rising at all. The next thing that could change that is not a rate decision. It is the Strait reopening while the rest of the settings stay where they are.

The instrument does not predict the Fed, the Strait, or the price of oil. It does one narrow thing: it says, each quarter, whether the binding scarcity is money pretending to be things. Right now it is not, and the movement toward the line this quarter came from a real shortage receding rather than from money tightening, which is the opposite of what the July reading expected to be reporting. That is the value of publishing a number instead of an essay. An essay could have absorbed the difference. The number said minus 0.66 for this quarter, the quarter came in at minus 1.08, and the arithmetic of why is written above where anyone can check it.
