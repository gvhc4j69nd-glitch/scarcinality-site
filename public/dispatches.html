Scarcinal Dispatches

Where the theory meets the tape. The framework makes conditional predictions; this is where they are written down, dated, and checked against what actually happens.

A theory that only explains the past is hard to be wrong about. Scarcinality tries to do more than that: it says, given a particular configuration, which way the binding scarcity should move and where it should show up first. The dispatches hold it to that. Three formats run here.

The standing ledger

One living document, updated rather than replaced, listing the framework's falsifiable claims and tracking each against reality. It is meant to let the theory be wrong out loud.

Each claim is a hypothesis, not a forecast. The point of dating them is to make it impossible to quietly revise the theory after the fact. The Toilet Paper Theory

A wealthy household does not use more toilet paper. A household near the poverty line goes without. Necessities carry a physical ceiling income cannot raise, so moving a dollar upward destroys demand for ordinary goods that the recipient structurally cannot replace. An extension to the framework, and the third place in two months where an aggregate instrument proved unable to see the bottom of a distribution.

Extending the Toilet Paper Theory

  • The Volume Tell. The theory’s most immediately testable prediction. If necessity demand is capped per person, staples firms should be posting revenue growth from price while unit volumes fall, with the difference going to private label and to the discounters. The 2026 sector language is already “volume-driven pricing elasticity”, which is a euphemism for exactly this. Quarterly data, no waiting required.
  • The Ceiling That Isn’t. The inverse case, and the sharper test. Housing and health care are necessities with no satiation ceiling in dollar terms: you can always be charged more for the same shelter and the same treatment. So instead of volume falling, price rises to absorb whatever income exists. That is why these two consume 41.6 percent of the bottom quintile’s spending, and why the theory predicts opposite behaviour in them.
  • The Hours Ceiling. The supply-side mirror. A person can sell only so many hours, so labour has a satiation ceiling too, and the visible edge of it is multiple job holding rather than higher wages.

Live conditions

  • Minsky’s Circle. The AI capital expenditure boom read as the financing taxonomy it actually is. Data centre capex above $1 trillion in 2026, roughly $4.1 trillion of associated debt financing, more than $800 billion in circular arrangements where the vendor funds its own customer, and Alphabet turning free cash flow negative for the first time. The Bank for International Settlements has named it among the three largest risks to global financial stability. Hedge to speculative to Ponzi, in public, with the migration documented in press releases.
  • The Tariff Is a Consumption Tax. The Toilet Paper Theory applied to trade policy. A tariff on ordinary goods is regressive by construction, and the theory says its cost does not appear as a uniform price rise but as destroyed volume at the bottom while the top absorbs it invisibly. Ties the current surcharge regime to a measurable mechanism rather than to a forecast.
  • The Frozen Market. Housing as the cleanest counterfeit currently on the board. Roughly 80 percent of outstanding mortgages sit below the market rate, existing sales run near 4.09 million against a population that wants houses, and the houses physically exist. Nothing has run out. A lock-in is a monetary artefact wearing the costume of a supply shortage.

Instruments and method

  • The Instruments Measure the Top. Three findings in two months converged on one defect: the Credit Counterfeit Index reads loose while subprime posts a thirty-two year record, no aggregate explains the divergence between corporate profits and small-firm conditions, and the federal survey of whether Americans go without is being discontinued. A proposal for a distributional gauge, and an argument that an economics built on averages reports health for as long as the people holding most of the dollars are healthy.
  • Stablecoins Do Not Escape the Dollar. Why a claim on dollars amplifies the very scarcity it claims to abstract away. Now a larger bet than when the treatise made it, at more than $321 billion and with a federal charter behind it.
  • The Captured Manager. Narrowed by what has already been scored. That degrading the lender of last resort is priced in advance is no longer the open question; it was observed. What remains is the harder one: what a pre-discounted central bank can actually do when a trigger arrives, and whether credibility spent in calm can be reassembled in a panic.
