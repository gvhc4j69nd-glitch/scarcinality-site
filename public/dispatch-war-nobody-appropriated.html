The War Nobody Appropriated

Congress spent three years arguing about the money for Ukraine. Every dollar was authorized, itemized and voted on. The other conflict, the one costing the world ten times as much, arrived without a single appropriation and without a vote, and that turns out to be the most interesting thing about the comparison.

Three claims: the two costs are different categories and should not share a ledger, the Ukraine spending was too small to measurably help or hurt the American economy, and the Iran conflict is an order of magnitude larger and lands hardest on countries that had no say in it. This is an accounting exercise, not an argument that either policy was right. Conditional and falsifiable.

Here are two numbers that belong in the same sentence and almost never appear in one.

Since February 2022, the United States has actually paid out $121.2 billion in support of Ukraine. That is across four and a half years, three Congresses and an enormous amount of argument.

The Iran conflict began on 28 February of this year. In the eight months since, it has cost the world economy somewhere near $1.3 trillion.

Ten times as much. In a fifth of the time. And nobody voted for a penny of it.

The Ukraine ledger, honestly

Start with what was authorized rather than what was spent, because the two are not close.

Congress appropriated $196.4 billion for the Ukraine response between 2022 and the middle of this year. Of that, $180.9 billion was obligated and $121.2 billion had actually gone out the door by 30 June.

The shape of the spending matters more than the total. The big supplemental was $60.78 billion in April 2024. After that it nearly stops. Five point one billion in fiscal 2025. Seven point six in fiscal 2026.

So this is not an ongoing program of the size people argue about. It is a large burst three years ago and a trickle since.

What America actually spent

The comparison so far has a flaw, and it is better fixed than hidden.

Ukraine appears above as American appropriations. Iran appears as world output. Those are different units, and putting them on one axis flatters the Iran side of the ledger.

So here is the like-for-like figure. US Central Command puts the cost of the Iran war at $43.6 billion through early September.

That includes about $28 billion to replace munitions. It excludes damage to American facilities across the region. The Congressional Budget Office had it at $38 billion through 1 August, running near $3 billion a month since.

Now annualize both sides, which is the only fair way to read them.

Ukraine support has averaged about $28 billion a year since February 2022. The Iran war is running near $84 billion a year.

Three times the rate, on a conflict eight months old.

And the appropriation came afterward. The Pentagon asked Congress for a supplemental, originally above $200 billion, since trimmed to somewhere between $80 and $100 billion.

The money was spent first and the vote was requested later. That is the reverse of how every Ukraine dollar moved.

Meanwhile the Ukraine line for fiscal 2026 is $400 million. At the Iran war’s monthly burn rate, that is about four days.

Now the scale check, which is where the argument usually goes wrong in both directions.

Net interest on the federal debt ran $1.052 trillion in fiscal 2026. That is about $2.9 billion a day.

Divide the Ukraine disbursements by that. Everything America has actually paid out since February 2022 equals roughly six weeks of interest on money already borrowed.

The entire Ukraine expenditure, the thing that shut down the House for weeks and split a governing coalition, is forty-two days of debt service. It was never a fiscal event. It was an argument about something else.

Did it help the American economy?

This is the question the dispatch was asked, and the honest answer is unsatisfying. It did not meaningfully help. It also did not meaningfully hurt. It was too small to do either.

The strongest version of the case for help goes like this. Roughly ninety percent of the money stays in the United States, because it buys weapons from American factories or replaces American stockpiles. The State Department made that claim repeatedly. More than seventy American cities have defense manufacturing funded by it, across at least thirty-eight states.

All of that is broadly true and none of it establishes what it is usually used to establish.

Where a dollar lands is not the same question as what a dollar does. The test is the multiplier: how much output you get per dollar spent.

For defense procurement the research is consistent and unflattering. The OECD puts it near 0.8. Goldman Sachs estimates 0.5 over two years. The broad literature clusters between 0.6 and 1.0. That is the low end of government spending generally. Weapons manufacturing is capital intensive, and it generates fewer jobs per dollar than almost any transfer program.

So run the arithmetic. The disbursements average about $27 billion a year. At a multiplier of 0.7 that is roughly $19 billion of output in an economy near $30 trillion.

Six hundredths of one percent. That is not a stimulus. That is a rounding error with a press release.

And reporting through the period found the job creation slower than promised, because building new production lines takes years rather than quarters. The factories are real. The timeline was oversold.

A program can be strategically significant and economically invisible at the same time. Ukraine support is the clearest recent example of both being true at once.

The Iran ledger is a different kind of thing

Here is where the comparison stops being an accounting exercise.

The $43.6 billion is the part that lands in a budget. It is not the part that matters most.

There is no line item anywhere for the $1.3 trillion. No legislature voted on it, and that is not because it is hidden. It is because it is not that kind of cost.

Ukraine support moves money from taxpayers to contractors. The money still exists afterward. It changes hands.

The Iran conflict destroys the delivery of a physical good. A barrel that does not move through the Strait is not transferred to anyone. It is simply absent from the world that needed it.

And the scale of the absence is historic. The closure has stranded roughly 75 percent of Gulf and Iraqi energy exports. That is the largest supply disruption since the Arab oil embargo of 1973.

The frictions underneath are extraordinary in their own right. War risk insurance for a tanker transiting Hormuz ran 0.15 to 0.25 percent of hull value before the war. It now runs 6 to 10 percent for a single voyage. On a $100 million ship that is a move from about $250,000 to between six and ten million. For one trip. It works out near $7 to $8 on every barrel. Tanker freight peaked around $140 a metric ton in March against a five year average of $18.91.

Who actually pays

Now the finding that should change how an American reads all of this.

The Dallas Fed has modeled a 15 percent global oil supply disruption. It cuts annualized real growth in the United States by about 0.3 percentage points. It cuts growth in the rest of the world by about 1.7.

Nearly six times the damage, everywhere else.

The reason is structural. America became a net energy exporter. A higher oil price takes money out of one American pocket and puts it in another, and the country as a whole is close to insulated. An importing economy has no second pocket.

The United States is the only major economy for which this conflict is mostly a price story rather than a supply story. That is exactly why it is being underestimated in the place that has the most influence over it.

The scarcinality reading

The framework asks one question of every shortage. Is the thing gone, or present and out of reach?

Ukraine support never made anything scarce. Not steel, not labor, not capacity. It moved money, and money is the one input a sovereign issuer never physically runs out of. The constraint was always political will, which is a decision wearing the costume of a limit.

The Iran conflict made something genuinely scarce. Oil that sits behind a closed strait is not available at any price to the refinery that needed it on Tuesday. That is the rare case this site keeps flagging as the real one. It is why the Credit Counterfeit Index sits at −1.36, with its real term at 1.35 doing every bit of the work.

Which produces the uncomfortable conclusion.

The cheaper conflict, the reversible one, the one that consumed years of legislative attention, was the monetary one. The expensive conflict is the irreversible one, the one actually rearranging the world economy. It got no appropriation debate at all, because it never needed an appropriation.

Democratic scrutiny attaches to spending, not to consequences. We examine what we authorize and wave through what we merely cause, and the second category is where the larger number has been hiding all year.

What would prove this wrong

Three tests.

Suppose the Strait reopens and growth recovers toward 3.4 percent, with the lost output never made up. Then the $1.3 trillion was a timing effect rather than a destruction, and this comparison overstates the Iran side.

If employment from Ukraine replenishment turns out materially larger than the multiplier literature predicts, the claim here fails. Economically invisible is a testable statement.

And the US growth hit may yet converge on the rest of the world rather than staying near a sixth of it. That would make the net exporter insulation argument wrong, and the asymmetry at the center of this piece would go with it.

Cautions, and they are substantial. The $1.3 trillion is a scenario-based estimate of output loss, not a measured figure. Credible ranges run from about $590 billion to well over $3 trillion, depending on duration. The Ukraine disbursement total is solid because it comes from appropriations tracking. The $43.6 billion is a Central Command estimate through early September and excludes damage to American facilities, so read it as a floor. The annualized figures are the fair comparison. The opening juxtaposition of a four-year outlay against a single-year output loss is not, which is why the piece corrects it on the way through. The ninety percent domestic share is a State Department figure and has been disputed. Nothing here argues that either policy was correct. It argues only that they are different kinds of cost and that one of them was examined far more carefully than its size warranted.
