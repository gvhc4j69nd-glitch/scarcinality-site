What a Denial Rate Hides

Start with the number that gets quoted. In 2024 traditional Medicare denied 22.9 percent of the prior authorization requests it reviewed. Medicare Advantage, run by private insurers, denied 7.7 percent. On the rate alone the government program looks three times harsher than the companies.

The rate is correct and the conclusion is backwards, because a rate is a ratio and the denominator is the policy. Traditional Medicare requires prior authorization for a short list: some outpatient procedures like blepharoplasty and vein ablation, repetitive non-emergency ambulance trips, and about seventy categories of durable medical equipment. That produced 628,243 reviews in the whole fiscal year, roughly 0.02 per beneficiary. Medicare Advantage produced 52.8 million, about 1.7 per enrollee, and 99 percent of enrollees face it on something.

Multiply it out and the ranking flips. Traditional Medicare generates about 4.6 denials per thousand beneficiaries a year. Medicare Advantage generates about 131. Same program, same entitlement, same year, and on the order of thirty times the chance that something a doctor ordered gets stopped before it happens.

Then the private markets against each other. KFF pulled 2025 filings from fourteen insurers with real market share and found standard denial rates of 12 percent in Medicare Advantage, 14 percent in Medicaid managed care and 18 percent in the ACA Marketplace. The spread inside each market is wider than the spread between them. Marketplace insurers run from 3 percent at GuideWell to 25 percent at Centene.

The cleanest way to see what is driving it is to follow one company. UnitedHealth Group is the only insurer in the analysis operating in all three markets. It denied 17 percent in Medicare Advantage, 11 percent in Medicaid and 21 percent in the Marketplace. One underwriting department, one year, one actuarial staff. The number moves with the regulator.

The appeal data says the same thing from the other side. Medicare Advantage denies the least and gets overturned the most, 67 percent of the time. The Marketplace denies the most and gets overturned the least, 43 percent. That looks like a paradox until you notice that Medicare Advantage is the only one of the three where a denial the insurer upholds goes to an independent reviewer automatically. Where the referee is strongest the denial rate is lowest, and the denials that do get made are the ones least likely to stand.

Almost nobody tests it. Enrollees appealed fewer than 1 percent of denied in-network Marketplace claims in 2024, and 66 percent of those appeals were upheld against them. In Medicare Advantage 11.5 percent of denials were appealed and 80.7 percent of those were overturned in whole or in part. Four out of five. A denial that fails four times out of five on review, and is reviewed one time in nine, is not functioning as a judgment about care. It is functioning as a toll on persistence.

The claims side, separately. Prior authorization happens before the service. Denial of a submitted claim happens after, and it is a different number. Marketplace insurers denied 19 percent of in-network claims in 2024, about 85 million out of 451 million, and 37 percent of out-of-network claims. Issuers ranged from 3 to 36 percent. Elevance had the lowest parent-company average at 8 percent and Oscar the highest at 25.

The reason codes on those denials are worth a look. Medical necessity, the thing everyone argues about, accounts for 5 percent. Administrative reasons account for 25. The single largest category, at 36 percent, is "other," which is a federal reporting field that permits an insurer to decline a claim without stating a classifiable reason. The most common reason for a denial in the American individual market is no reason.

The scarcinality reading. This page is the framework's cleanest case, because nothing is scarce anywhere in it. The surgeon exists. The scanner exists and has open slots. The drug is in the pharmacy. The patient is insured and has paid the premium. Every physical input to the care is present, and the care does not happen. What intervenes is a ledger entry, and the rate at which that entry gets made is not set by medicine. It moves by twelve percentage points depending on which agency is reading the filings. That is a manufactured shortage in the strictest sense the theory allows: an interruption with no physical correlate at all, sitting one level above the thing it interrupts.

It also explains why the cost argument never resolves. Prior authorization is defended as a brake on unnecessary spending, and some of it certainly is. But a brake applied 52.8 million times a year, which fails four appeals in five when anybody pushes back, is not being used only as a brake. The 11.5 percent appeal rate is the operative statistic. The system works, financially, because most people stop.

How much to trust these numbers. The three panels use two different KFF analyses and should not be read across. Panel one is 2024 federal data covering all of Medicare. Panels two and three are 2025 filings from fourteen insurers above a 2.5 percent share threshold, which is the first year this reporting existed and is thin: CVS and several Marketplace issuers could not be located at all, and Medicaid fee-for-service is excluded because state reports are scattered and the populations are not comparable. Denials per thousand is our arithmetic on KFF's published per-enrollee and rate figures, not a figure KFF publishes, and it inherits their rounding. KFF gives traditional Medicare as "about 0.02" requests per beneficiary, so the multiple between the two programs is good to roughly twenty-five to thirty rather than to a decimal place. The direction is not in doubt. The precision is. And the deepest limitation is one nobody can fix from filings: none of this says whether a denial was right. A 22.9 percent rate on blepharoplasty and a 7.7 percent rate on skilled nursing stays are not the same act, and no public data set grades them. Arguments welcome.
