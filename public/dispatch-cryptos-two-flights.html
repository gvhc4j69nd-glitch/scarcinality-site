Crypto’s Two Flights

Crypto has no fixed role in a crisis. It has two opposite ones, and which it plays is decided by the same thing that decides everything in this framework: which scarcity is biting. Three episodes fit the claim cleanly. The most recent one does not, and the reason it does not is the most interesting thing on this page.

Section X of the treatise made this argument in 2026 on a short record. This dispatch scores it against the evidence, including a recent episode that does not fit, and updates the standing ledger accordingly. Conditional, falsifiable, not investment advice.

The question is nearly always put the wrong way. Is crypto a safe haven or a risk asset? Is Bitcoin digital gold or a leveraged bet on liquidity? Both sides marshal episodes, both sides are sometimes right, and the argument never resolves because it assumes the answer is a property of the asset. The framework refuses that premise. Crypto has no fixed role in a crisis. It has two opposite roles, and which one it plays is decided not by what crypto is but by what people are running from.

The two flights

The first flight is toward the dollar. In an acute dash for cash, the lower-right quadrant of the phase plane, the master-scarce asset is the dollar, for the unanswerable reason that debts are denominated in dollars. A margin call, a maturing bond, a mortgage payment: each is an obligation to deliver dollars, and no quantity of a token discharges it. So a unit scrambling for liquidity does not flee into crypto. It sells crypto to raise the dollars it actually owes. And because crypto sits at the summit of Minsky’s fragility stack, the most speculative and most leveraged position in the system, it is not the last thing liquidated but the first. In that regime, distance from the dollar is vulnerability rather than refuge.

The second flight is away from the dollar. In the upper-right quadrant, or more precisely in any regime where the thing in doubt is the value of money itself, what has gone scarce is not dollars but trust in dollars. A central bank bent to fiscal will, a deficit financed by the printing press, a unit of account losing credibility. Here the flight runs outward, and an asset outside the system can catch the outflow. The digital-gold thesis, false in a liquidity panic, finds its hour exactly when the dollar is the thing in question.

The same asset, the same week-to-week chart, means opposite things depending on which scarcity sits at the top of the rank. That is not a hedge failing or working. It is one instrument reading two different diseases.

The episode that does not fit, and why it matters

The framework’s second flight predicts that when the dollar’s credibility is in question, an asset outside the dollar system catches the outflow. Through 2025 the conditions for that flight were as favourable as they have been in decades. The deficit was enormous, the central bank was under sustained and public political pressure, and the debasement argument was being made loudly by exactly the people who hold this asset. The flight did not happen. Bitcoin instead traded like unprofitable technology stock, correlation with software equities tightening to +0.64 by the fourth quarter, its highest reading since the 2022 bear market. Its negative correlation with the dollar, which ran near minus 0.4 in 2022 and 2023, decayed toward zero.

An honest framework has to sit with that rather than explain it away. Three readings are available and they are not mutually exclusive.

The first is that the flight has a threshold and it was not crossed. Debasement worry is not the same as debasement. A currency whose credibility is questioned in op-eds is still functioning as the unit of account, and until it visibly stops doing so, holders have no operational reason to leave. On this reading the framework is intact and simply untested, which is a comfortable conclusion and therefore one to distrust.

The second is that crypto’s ownership changed. The asset that rallied on debasement in 2021 was held largely by people who believed a monetary thesis. The asset held in 2026 sits in exchange-traded funds, on institutional balance sheets, inside portfolios managed against equity benchmarks and margined alongside equity positions. An asset held by leveraged allocators trades like the rest of a leveraged allocator’s book, whatever it was designed to be. Its beta is now a fact about its holders rather than about its protocol.

The third is the one the framework finds most interesting, and it is a scarcinal argument. In July 2025 the GENIUS Act created a federal framework for stablecoin reserves, and the stablecoin market has since grown to more than $321 billion, of which roughly 99 percent is denominated in dollars and increasingly backed by Treasuries. Consider what that does to the second flight. Someone worried about the dollar’s management, who five years ago had to leave the dollar system entirely to act on that worry, can now hold a regulated, federally blessed, Treasury-backed dollar token instead. The escape route was absorbed into the thing it was an escape from.

The state did not close the exit. It built a licensed one that leads back inside, and the outflow took it.

That is a mechanism the treatise did not anticipate, and it is the sort the framework should be able to name once it appears. Section X argued that stablecoins do not escape the dollar because a claim on dollars is a leveraged bet that dollars remain available. That remains true and remains the risk. But the same property that makes stablecoins dangerous in a dash for cash makes them effective at absorbing a flight from the currency, because to the holder they feel like an exit while to the system they are additional dollar demand. Roughly $321 billion of what might otherwise have been a flight from the dollar is now, in substance, a bid for Treasuries.

Where the gauge points now

The framework’s own instrument gives a clear reading, and it is the reason the current period looks so unlike 2020 or 2023. The Credit Counterfeit Index sits at minus 1.31 as of the second quarter of 2026, well below its threshold and deeply negative, meaning the monetary bind is loose relative to the real one. Neither flight is active. There is no dash for cash to sell into and no visible collapse of monetary trust to run from. In that condition the framework predicts exactly what the market is doing: with neither scarcity binding, crypto has no signal to read and reverts to trading as high-beta risk, which is its behaviour in the absence of either flight rather than a refutation of both.

This is a genuinely awkward place for the theory to sit, because it means the framework currently predicts the least interesting outcome. A claim that only becomes testable in a crisis is a claim that spends most of its life unfalsifiable. The response is not to reach for a crisis but to state in advance, precisely, what each one would look like, which is what the table below is for.

The claim this dispatch defends is narrower than the one usually argued about. It is not that crypto is a hedge, and not that it is not. It is that the question has no answer in the abstract, because the asset is a high-beta instrument inside the ordering of scarcities rather than an escape from it, and it amplifies whichever way the rank is moving. Sold when dollars are scarce. Bought when trust in dollars is scarce. Directionless when neither is, which is where we are now and which is why it currently trades like a technology stock.

The genuinely new thing in the record is not that crypto failed a test. It is that the second flight now has a competing destination, licensed by statute, denominated in the currency being fled, and holding Treasuries against its liabilities. If that holds, the framework’s original claim survives in form but loses much of its force in practice, because the outflow it predicted will keep arriving somewhere that is, on inspection, still inside the dollar.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
