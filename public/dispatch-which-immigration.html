Which Immigration

Whether immigration helps or hurts is a malformed question in the same way that whether spending is good is malformed. Break it apart and the pieces point somewhere specific, and not where the argument usually goes. The aggregate has been positive throughout the period in which the politics got steadily worse, which tells you the aggregate was never what anyone was responding to.

The first policy-facing dispatch on this site, and a departure worth flagging. The framework normally reads conditions and declines to prescribe. Here it is asked what would follow if its readings are right, so the measures below are stated as consequences of the analysis rather than as advocacy, and the section that matters most is the one listing what would falsify them. Companion to the aid and migration comparison. Not policy advice.

Sort the evidence by how settled it is rather than by how often it is quoted, and the shape of the disagreement changes. Most of what is argued about is not what is uncertain, and most of what is uncertain is barely argued about at all.

The fourth band is the honest one. Wage effects on native workers without a high school diploma are not settled, the disagreement between Borjas and Card is real rather than partisan noise, and this dispatch is not going to pretend otherwise. If those effects are meaningful, some of the gains are landing on exactly the households the Toilet Paper Theory is about, and that is a serious objection rather than a talking point.

The design flaw underneath the politics

The third band contains the finding that explains almost everything about how this argument is conducted, and it is not really about immigration at all.

Costs:  schools, emergency rooms, local services
        land on states and counties, immediately

Revenue: income and payroll tax, compounding with tenure
        lands on the federal government, over decades

A county commissioner and the Congressional Budget Office
look at the same person and both reach correct conclusions.

That is not a communications failure or a public that has been misled. It is an accounting mismatch, and accounting mismatches are fixable without changing a single admission number. Any analysis that treats local opposition as irrational has skipped the only part of the arithmetic that local governments can see.

Where this framework has something of its own to say

Most of the above is standard economics. The contribution this framework makes is different and comes from the hours ceiling.

The framework’s method is to ask which shortage is real. Applied across recent dispatches it has kept finding, unusually, that the answer is real rather than counterfeit: 36,000 artillery shells against a target of 100,000, roughly 140,000 skilled shipyard workers who do not exist, electricians named by Microsoft as the single largest constraint on data center construction, transformer manufacturing at 144-week lead times. Those are hours ceilings. They do not answer money on the timescale money is being spent.

Immigration is the only input that relieves an hours ceiling faster than a training pipeline, because a trained welder can arrive next month and a new one takes years. That is not a moral argument. It is the same arithmetic that made the munitions case a real scarcity rather than a counterfeit one.

Which means the framework, applied honestly, is more favorable to immigration on the skilled-trades margin than most commentary, and it gets there without any appeal to values. It also implies something narrower than the usual pro-immigration position: the case is strongest precisely where a documented physical bottleneck exists, and it is much weaker where it does not.

Six measures that follow

Reimburse local costs federally. The federal government captures the long-run revenue, so it should carry the short-run cost. This changes admission levels by zero and removes most of the rational local objection. Highest ratio of political benefit to economic cost of anything available, and it is pure accounting.
Compress the delay before work authorization. Every month between arrival and lawful work converts a future taxpayer into a present cost, benefiting nobody. This is the largest single driver of near-term fiscal drag and it is entirely self-inflicted.
Allocate a channel against documented occupational shortages. Not by degree level and not by country of origin, but against measured bottlenecks: welders, electricians, linemen, transformer technicians, the trades whose absence is currently stopping physical construction. The hours ceiling is what makes this different from a general skills preference.
Buy predictability rather than a number. School districts and employers can absorb almost any steady rate and cannot absorb volatility. Surge costs are front-loaded against fixed capacity. A boring announced figure outperforms a higher average delivered in waves.
Make the lawful channel beat the smuggler on price and time. The binding constraint on orderly migration is not enforcement appetite. It is that the unlawful route is currently faster and cheaper for most applicants. A channel that wins on those two dimensions redirects flow without enforcing anything.
Cut the cost of sending money home. Remittances cost 6.36 percent to send globally against a 3 percent target. Closing that gap returns roughly $20 billion a year to origin-country households at no cost to the United States, which is about thirty times the aid budget it would substitute for.

Note what these have in common. Five of the six work by moving costs to where the benefits already are, or by removing a delay, rather than by changing how many people arrive. The level is the thing the argument is about and very nearly the only lever the analysis does not reach for.

Where this diverges from what is actually on the table

Two things define the current legislative position. The Secure America Act, enacted in June 2026 through reconciliation, directs $69.5 billion to Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Customs and Border Protection through 2029. The Dignity Act, introduced in July 2025 by Salazar and Escobar with bipartisan cosponsors, is the serious comprehensive proposal still pending.

The Dignity Act deserves credit where the analysis above agrees with it, and it agrees more than the framework expected.

The corrective, stated plainly

Three divergences matter and the rest are detail.

First, the fiscal transfer is missing from everything. No bill on the table sends federal money to the counties carrying the near-term cost, and the federal government is the party collecting the eventual revenue. This is the cheapest available fix for the loudest available grievance and nobody has proposed it, which is strange enough to be worth saying twice.

Second, the visa reforms answer the wrong question. Raising per-country caps from 7 to 15 percent and excluding derivatives from the count are sensible repairs to a queue. They are not an allocation mechanism. A country facing a documented shortage of linemen does not need a shorter queue for the same applicants, it needs a channel pointed at linemen. This is the one place where the framework generates a proposal that is not already in circulation, and it follows from the hours ceiling rather than from any view about immigration.

Third, $69.5 billion was spent on one blade of a pair of scissors. Enforcement raises the cost of the unlawful route. It does not lower the cost of the lawful one. If the binding constraint is relative price and relative speed, spending on only one side moves the ratio less than spending the same money across both, and the framework’s standing question applies to the appropriation exactly as it applied to munitions: the money was found, and the question is whether the thing it was buying answers a bid.

The country is arguing about how many. The analysis says the recoverable gains are almost entirely in which, when, and who pays. Those are three separate questions and none of them is the one on the ballot.

What this does not settle

The framework can say that the aggregate is positive, that the distribution is a design choice, and that certain measures are positive-sum on the available evidence. It cannot say how to weigh a large gain to a migrant against a small loss to a native worker. That is a values question, and no quantity of statistical work resolves it. Anyone claiming their spreadsheet answers it is smuggling a moral premise past the reader.

Three caveats. This dispatch prescribes, which the framework normally refuses to do, and the reader should discount it accordingly: reading conditions and recommending action are different activities with different error rates. The wage evidence in the fourth band is genuinely unresolved and the measures above are more robust to that uncertainty than a pure expansion argument would be, but they are not immune to it. And legislative provisions have been read from published summaries and section-by-section documents rather than from bill text, so a detail may have moved.

The treatise asks which shortage is real. Applied here the answer is unusual, because it points in two directions at once. The shortage of skilled hands in the trades is real, and immigration is the only instrument that relieves it inside a decade. The shortage of money in the counties absorbing new arrivals is counterfeit, because the revenue exists and is simply arriving at a different address. One of those is fixed by admitting people. The other is fixed by a transfer nobody has proposed. Neither is fixed by arguing about the number.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
