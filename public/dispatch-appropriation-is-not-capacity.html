The Appropriation Is Not the Capacity

This framework nearly always finds that the binding shortage is monetary, that nothing real has run out, and that the scarcity of goods is a costume worn by a scarcity of claims. Government spending in wartime is the case that runs the other way, and it runs the other way for an instructive reason. A sovereign borrowing in its own currency has already proved that money is not its constraint. What is left, when the money is definitionally available, is whatever was actually scarce all along.

A themed reading against the treatise, and the second time the framework has found a genuine rather than a counterfeit shortage. The first was the Hormuz closure. The claim here is that fiscal expansion aimed at a physically bounded sector does not purchase output, it purchases price, and that the price escapes the sector through shared inputs. Conditional, falsifiable, not policy advice.

Through the first ten months of fiscal 2026 the United States government spent $6.284 trillion, collected $4.485 trillion, and borrowed the $1.799 trillion difference. Total public debt outstanding reached $39.93 trillion on 13 August, of which $32.2 trillion is held by the public, and the Congressional Budget Office expects debt held by the public to pass the 1946 record of 106 percent of GDP before the decade is out. National defense was funded at roughly $1.045 trillion, the largest peacetime allocation in the country's history and a jump of more than 17 percent in a single year.

Every one of those dollars was found. Not one appropriation failed for want of money. This is the ordinary condition of a government that borrows in a currency it issues, and it is usually discussed as a warning. Here it is useful for a different reason: it makes the period a clean experiment. If money is never the binding scarcity for the sovereign, then whatever went wrong in the sector it was spending into cannot be a monetary problem. The framework's habitual answer is unavailable, which means the framework has to accept whatever the evidence says instead.

What the evidence says is that the money arrived and the shells did not.

The deliveries, against the money

The Army set a goal of 100,000 rounds of 155 millimetre artillery per month, to be reached by October 2025. As of March 2026, according to the Department of Defense Inspector General, production stood at roughly 36,000 a month. That is genuine growth, since the pre-2022 rate was near 14,500, and it is also a little over a third of the target, eighteen months late. A contractor-operated plant in Mesquite, Texas, built at a cost of $469 million, had not produced a single shell meeting Army specification.

The consumption side is starker. Roughly 319 Tomahawk missiles were expended in the first six days of the campaign against Iran. Scheduled deliveries of Tomahawks for the whole of fiscal 2026 are 190. Six days of use consumed more than a year and a half of production. Patriot deliveries for the year run to 172 missiles. THAAD interceptors have seen no new deliveries at all since August 2023, with production not resuming until April 2027.

Behind all of it sits an input problem that predates every one of these decisions. The United States has not produced TNT domestically since 1986. Supply comes from allies, chiefly Australia and India. The first new domestic plant, in Graham, Kentucky, is scheduled for completion in February 2029, and its output is designated for military requirements with none earmarked for commercial industry.

Why this is the framework inverted

Section IV of the treatise describes a monetary scarcity descending and counterfeiting a scarcity of goods. The test that distinguishes a counterfeit from a genuine shortage is simple and it is the one the framework applies everywhere: does the constraint answer a bid? If money appears and the goods follow, then goods were never short and the shortage was monetary. If money appears and the goods do not follow, the shortage is real.

By that test the munitions case is not close. The bid was made, at scale, publicly, with bipartisan support and no financing constraint whatever, and the answer was 36,000 shells against 100,000, zero interceptors for three years, and a $469 million facility producing nothing that met specification. This is what the framework's own criterion calls a real scarcity. It would be dishonest to reach for the usual reading here, and the framework's default answer would have been wrong.

Counterfeit scarcity:  bid rises → quantity follows → the shortage was money
Genuine scarcity:    bid rises → quantity does not → the shortage was the thing

Bid: unconstrained, roughly $1.045 trillion.
Quantity: 36 percent of target, eighteen months late.
Verdict: genuine.

The interesting part is what happens next, because a genuine scarcity meeting an unconstrained bid does not simply fail quietly. The money does not evaporate. It is still in the system, still bidding, and it goes looking for the same bounded inputs through every available door.

The shock is the spillover, not the spending

Ask where a bid goes when the thing it wants cannot be produced faster, and the answer is that it goes into the price of whatever is upstream, and from there into every other user of the same upstream.

Energetics is the clearest channel. Explosives are not only a military product. Ammonium nitrate and TNT are what mining and quarrying run on, and quarrying is where aggregate comes from, and aggregate is what concrete is, and concrete is housing, roads, bridges, and data centres. When defence buyers compete for a limited tonnage the civilian market is priced out of the same tonnage, and the new domestic capacity being built to relieve the military shortage is explicitly not being built to relieve the civilian one. So a war in the Gulf raises the cost of a quarry in Pennsylvania, and the cost of the quarry raises the cost of a road, and none of that appears in any account as a war expense.

This is the descent running backwards. The framework's usual case is a monetary shortage that counterfeits a shortage of goods. Here a monetary abundance, aimed at a bounded thing, manufactures a genuine shortage of goods and exports it to everyone who shares the input.

The crowding out reached the buyer's own house as well, which is the detail that makes it hard to dismiss as an accounting artefact. Operation Epic Fury cost roughly $29 billion. In May the Chief of Naval Operations told Congress that from July he would have to begin changing training, operations, and certification events, meaning the service was deferring the activity that produces future readiness in order to fund present operations. The Gerald R. Ford accrues about 6 percent of additional maintenance for every 30 days of extended deployment, so a five-month extension carries something near 30 percent. None of that is a funding decision. It is a statement about hulls, dry docks, and the number of days in a year.

The ceiling underneath all of it

Follow every one of these bottlenecks down far enough and the same thing is at the bottom, which is the hours ceiling at national scale.

The Navy's submarine programmes need on the order of 140,000 skilled workers, roughly 100,000 for construction and 40,000 for maintenance, in welding, CNC machining, pipefitting, and nondestructive testing. Those people do not exist and cannot be appropriated into existence, because the constraint on their number is a training pipeline measured in years for the ordinary grades and in decades for the master ones. Electric Boat is hiring on the order of 8,000 people in 2026 and had passed 4,000 by late July, which is a serious effort and is also arithmetic that does not close the gap this decade. Virginia class boats now run an average of four years behind the schedules signed into their contracts, and the delays grew from 2025 to 2026 despite the money.

This is precisely the case the hours ceiling describes. Every other input answers a higher price by supplying more. Labour answers with the same quantity, because the seller of the hour and the hour are the same object, and a master welder is thirty years of hours that were either accumulated or were not. The submarines were paid for. They were not built, and the reason has nothing to do with whether they were paid for.

Who ends up holding the price

The distributional signature of all this is the one the Toilet Paper Theory predicts, and it is worth stating plainly because war spending is usually discussed as though its costs were shared.

The outlay side of a defence buildout converts borrowed dollars into contracts, and contracts into revenue, and revenue into earnings held by a concentrated shareholder base. The price side lands on inputs that ordinary production shares: explosives into aggregate into construction, machinists and welders bid away from civilian manufacturing, and borrowing that competes at the long end for the same savings that price a mortgage. A household in the top quintile receives the first effect and is insulated from the second, since necessity prices are a small share of its spending. A household near the poverty line receives only the second. The transfer runs uphill, and it runs uphill through the price of concrete.

The line that buys nothing

There is one item in the fiscal accounts that is larger than the entire military and purchases no capacity of any kind.

Through ten months of fiscal 2026 the government paid $1,169.6 billion in gross interest on the public debt, against $764.7 billion in outlays for all Department of Defense military programmes. Netting out the interest received by trust funds still leaves roughly $963 billion, which is more than the military. The Congressional Budget Office puts net interest at about $1.0 trillion in 2026 and $2.1 trillion by 2036, rising from 3.3 to 4.6 percent of GDP.

Interest is a pure claim. It commissions no shell, trains no welder, and pours no concrete. It is the accumulated price of past bids, and it grows on its own terms regardless of whether the bids ever produced anything. A country that spends more servicing what it borrowed than on the thing it borrowed for has arrived at the treatise's central asymmetry from the fiscal side: the claims layer compounds without limit while the productive layer is bounded by matter and by hours, and the gap between them is not closed by issuing more claims.

Gross interest exceeded all military spending by more than half, and it is the one line in the budget that cannot be delivered late, because there is nothing to deliver.

Two caveats, because this dispatch reaches further outside the framework's usual evidence than most. Wartime cost figures are estimates produced under time pressure by analysts working from public schedules, and the munitions numbers in particular should be read as the best available rather than as audited. And the framework has no view on whether any of this spending was warranted, which is a question about strategy and about ends that the framework is not equipped to answer and does not attempt to.

What it does have a view on is the mechanism. The treatise's recurring finding is that people are told a real thing has run out when the thing that has actually run out is money, and that the remedy is therefore easier than it appears. This case is the inversion, and it carries the inverse warning. When the money is genuinely unlimited and the thing is genuinely bounded, spending more does not produce more. It produces a higher price for a fixed quantity, a queue in front of the bottleneck, and a bill arriving in the price of everything else that needed the same input. The factories do not stand and the shelves are not full. That is a rarer condition than this framework usually finds, and it is the reason for saying so plainly when it does.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
