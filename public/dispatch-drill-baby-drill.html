Drill, Baby, Drill

It was a promise about rigs, so the honest test is whether the rigs turned up. The land really was opened, on a scale worth taking seriously. Then you set the leases granted against the holes actually drilled, and the gap between them turns out to be the most interesting number in American energy policy.

A companion to The Appropriation Is Not the Capacity and the rig and Brent series, which was extended to September 2026 from the EIA and Baker Hughes files this week. Three claims: the leases are being bought but barely used, 2025 isolates leases from price and finds leases wanting, and the drilling response to price has broken down compared with 2021. The policy is assessed against its own stated aim, which was more drilling. Conditional and falsifiable.

Drill, baby, drill was a promise about rigs. Not about auctions, or acreage, or receipts. About steel turning in the ground.

So the honest test is simple. Count the rigs.

In 2025 the American rig count averaged 562. In 2026, through September, it has averaged 564.

Two rigs.

That is the whole answer, and the rest of this dispatch is about why, because the usual explanations are wrong in an interesting way.

The land really was opened

Start by giving the policy its due, because the opening was not cosmetic.

In December 2025 the United States auctioned drilling rights across roughly 80 million acres of the Gulf of Mexico. It was the first offshore sale since 2023 and the first of thirty that Congress had just mandated through 2040.

Companies bid on 1.02 million acres.

One point three percent. The other ninety-eight point seven drew no bid at all.

Onshore, the money was real

Onshore the story is different, and the appetite was genuine.

Onshore, the Bureau of Land Management held 22 lease sales in 2025, leasing 369 parcels for $356.6 million. Expressions of interest are the industry asking for specific ground to be offered. They went from 94,361 acres in 2023 to more than 2.4 million in 2025.

This year the receipts jumped. First quarter sales brought $592.7 million, the second quarter $4.1 billion, the third $372.1 million. Call it $5.1 billion for the year, against $356.6 million the year before.

One caveat on that figure, because it matters. A single record sale in New Mexico and Texas accounted for more than $4 billion of it. Strip that one auction of Permian acreage out and the rest of the year looks a great deal like 2025.

The terms were also made cheaper. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act cut the minimum onshore royalty to 12.5 percent. It had been 16.67 under the Inflation Reduction Act.

Read 2025 again, because it is the closest thing to a controlled experiment this question is ever going to get.

Twenty-two lease sales. Expressions of interest up twenty-five fold. And the rig count fell from 582 in January to 546 in December.

Leases went up. Drilling went down.

So which one was it

Drilling did pick up in 2026. The rig count went from 544 in January to 593 in September.

Both candidate causes rose in that window, so the timing alone cannot settle it. Three things do.

First, 2025. Lease availability rose enormously while the price fell, and drilling fell with the price. If permission were the constraint, that year should have produced a boom. It produced a six percent decline.

Second, the lag. Brent jumped from $70.89 in February to $103.13 in March. The rig count in April was 545, lower than January. It began climbing in May and accelerated through July, which is the two to three month delay the series has shown for a decade. The record lease sale happened in May, after the turn had already started.

Third, the idle acreage. This is the one that closes the argument.

Bought, but not used

The question of whether the new leases have been taken up has two answers, and they differ by geography.

Offshore, barely. Three statutory sales have drawn bids on a fraction of what was offered. Receipts fell 84 percent between the first and the second.

Onshore, enthusiastically. The $5.1 billion is real money, and expressions of interest rising twenty-five fold is a genuine signal of appetite.

But appetite for a lease is not the same as intent to drill it.

Of the more than 26 million onshore acres already under federal lease, roughly 13.9 million are not producing anything. That is 53 percent. In the lower 48 the figure reaches 56 percent, about 20.8 million acres sitting idle.

Between 2011 and 2020, companies let more than 20 million acres of approved federal leases expire or lapse without ever drilling them.

And the permits tell the same story. Operators have been holding thousands of approved, unused permits to drill for years, around 6,653 of them at the most recent count.

You cannot be constrained by a shortage of permission while sitting on thirteen million acres of permission you have not used. Whatever is limiting American drilling, it is not the availability of ground.

So why buy more? Because a lease at 12.5 percent royalty is a cheap option on a future price. Options are worth holding even when you have no intention of exercising them soon. The acreage is an asset on a balance sheet before it is a hole in the ground.

The response that broke

There is a larger finding sitting underneath all of this, and it is visible in the extended series.

Compare two recoveries.

In 2021, Brent rose 35 percent across the year and the rig count rose 55 percent, from 378 to 586. The drilling response was larger than the price move that caused it.

In 2026, Brent has risen 71 percent and the rig count has risen 9 percent, from 544 to 593.

Twice the price signal. A sixth of the response.

Take the whole eleven year series. The month-to-month correlation between a change in Brent and a change in the rig count one or two months later is about 0.21. Positive, and weak. Price matters, but it has stopped commanding.

The scarcinality reading

A lease is a permission. The treatise is blunt about what those are worth. A permission is a claim on a resource, not the resource itself. The two get confused constantly.

This is the cleanest worked example the site has found.

The government can issue permission at will. It has issued a great deal of it, cheaply, and the industry has bought the onshore portion and ignored most of the offshore. Neither action has moved the thing the policy was meant to move.

Because the binding constraints are elsewhere, and all three are real. Capital discipline imposed by shareholders who punished the last boom. Crews and pressure pumping equipment that take quarters to assemble. And in 2026, a closed strait that no amount of drilling in New Mexico reopens.

Opening more land to drilling during a shortage caused by a blocked waterway is a policy aimed at the wrong scarcity. It is not that it fails. It is that it was never pointed at the problem.

What would prove this wrong

Three tests.

The fourth Gulf sale may attract bids on a materially larger share of what it offers. That would make the thin uptake a matter of terms or timing rather than appetite, and the offshore half of this argument weakens.

The non-producing share of leased onshore acreage may fall below about 40 percent in the next two years. That would mean companies are drilling what they hold, and the idle-acreage argument fails.

And the rig count may yet climb past 650 while Brent falls back under $90. That would mean something other than price is driving the rise, and leases become the leading candidate by elimination.

Cautions. The idle-acreage figures come from Interior’s own leasing review and are a few years old. They describe the structure of the problem rather than this month’s level. The direction has not changed, but the exact percentages will have. The unused permit count is from 2023. Lease receipts are a poor proxy for acreage, which is why the single $4 billion Permian sale is flagged rather than buried. And 2026 is nine months of rig data, not twelve. The strongest claim here is the 2025 one, where leases and price moved in opposite directions and drilling followed the price.
