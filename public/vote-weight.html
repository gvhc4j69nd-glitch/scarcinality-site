What a Vote Weighs

Each chamber applies a different rule to the same population. The House divides 435 seats among the states by population and rounds. The electoral college takes those seats and adds two per state. The Senate skips population entirely.

Electoral votes come from the 2020 census and will not change until 2032. Population is the Census Bureau estimate for 1 July 2025. The District of Columbia is shown in black.

The scale is logarithmic because a linear one makes forty states invisible. On a linear axis, Wyoming’s bar would be 1.5 percent the height of California’s.

The smallest states are unlikely to vote as a bloc, and in practice they do not. The curve is a floor on how little of the country a Senate outcome can rest on, not a prediction that it will.

Start with one state and three rules. Wyoming had 588,753 residents in July 2025. It gets one seat in the House, so that seat stands for 588,753 people. It gets three electoral votes, so each stands for 196,251. It gets two senators, so each stands for 294,376. Same state, same census, three answers.

Now California, with 39,355,309. Each of its 52 House seats stands for 756,833 people. Each of its 54 electoral votes stands for 728,802. Each of its two Senate seats stands for 19,677,654.

The spread is the whole story. Across all the states, a House seat ranges over a factor of 1.90. An electoral vote ranges over 4.04. A Senate seat ranges over 66.85. One country, one population, and the distortion grows seventeenfold as you move from the first chamber to the third.

Where the electoral college gets its tilt. A state’s electoral votes are its House seats plus two. Those two are the Senate leaking into the presidential election. For every state with a single House seat the bonus is worth exactly three times, because it turns one unit into three. For California it is worth about 4 percent, and for Texas about 5. The bonus is not a thumb on the scale. It is a multiplier for the seven smallest states and a rounding error for the largest.

The House is not clean either. It is supposed to be proportional, and 1.90 to one is not proportional. The cause is the cap. The Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 fixed the House at 435 seats, so every state’s share has to be rounded to a whole number out of a fixed total. Rhode Island gets two seats for 1,114,521 people, which is 557,260 each. Delaware gets one seat for 1,059,952. Montana and Idaho both have two seats, and Idaho has 77 percent more people per seat.

Which makes the legal position strange. In Karcher v. Daggett, decided in 1983, the Supreme Court struck down New Jersey’s congressional map. The largest district held 527,472 people and the smallest 523,798. The gap was 0.6984 percent, and the Court held there is no deviation small enough to be excused without justification. Delaware’s seat holds 90.2 percent more people than one of Rhode Island’s. That gap is about 129 times the one struck down, and it is not merely permitted. It is required, because district lines stop at the state border.

Texas is the worst-represented state, and that is an accident of timing. Each of its 40 electoral votes stands for 792,746 people, the highest in the country. Its 38 House seats were set by the 2020 census, when its apportionment population was 29,183,290. It has added about 2.5 million people since. Nothing adjusts until the 2030 census takes effect in 2032. A growing state is underrepresented for a decade by construction.

Washington D.C. is the other direction. It has 693,645 residents, more than Wyoming and more than Vermont. The Twenty-Third Amendment gives it three electoral votes, the same as the smallest state. It has no senators and no voting representative. On the presidential scale it is the third best represented unit in the country. On the Senate scale it does not appear.

So how much does the Senate bonus actually decide? Twice since 1888 a candidate has won the presidency while losing the popular vote. The two cases answer differently, and the arithmetic is simple enough to check by hand. Strip the two senatorial electors from every state and give the District nothing, and 435 votes remain.

In 2000, Bush carried 30 states and 271 pledged electors, Gore 20 states plus the District and 267. Removing the bonus leaves Bush 211, Gore 224. Gore wins, and he had already won the popular vote. The bonus decided that election.

In 2016, Trump carried 30 states and 306 pledged electors, Clinton 20 states plus the District and 232. Removing the bonus leaves Trump 246, Clinton 189. Trump still wins comfortably, having lost the popular vote by 2.87 million. The bonus did not decide that one. Winner take all did, by converting narrow state wins into whole blocs.

Both counts sum to 435, which is the check that the state tallies are right. The lesson is that the electoral college has two separate distortions, and the famous one is the smaller one.

The Senate needs no such counterfactual. Rank the states from smallest to largest and add up the people behind the seats. Thirty-four seats, enough to block a treaty or sustain any veto, sit behind 7.4 percent of the population. Forty-two seats, enough to hold a filibuster, sit behind 11.2 percent. A working majority of 52 sits behind 17.5 percent. Sixty-eight seats, enough to override any veto, sit behind 31.5 percent.

Those are floors, not forecasts. The small states do not vote as a bloc and never have. The number says what a Senate result can rest on, not what it does rest on.

What would change it. Enlarging the House is the only one of these that does not need an amendment, since the 435 is a statute. Size the House so the average district matches the smallest state and it grows to about 579 seats. That cuts the electoral college spread from 4.04 to 2.91. It does not touch the Senate, because the Senate is not a function of population at all. Article V protects it twice over: equal suffrage in the Senate is the one clause the amendment process cannot reach without a state’s own consent.

Method. Electoral votes and House seats are from the Census Bureau’s 2020 apportionment, in force for 2024 and 2028. Population is the Bureau’s vintage 2025 estimate for 1 July 2025, which is why the ratios differ slightly from ones computed on census day. Apportionment population includes overseas federal employees and resident population does not, so the 2020 and 2025 figures are not quite the same quantity. The counterfactuals use pledged electors rather than the votes actually cast, which is why 2016 reads 306 and 232 rather than 304 and 227.

A note on what this is not. None of this shows the design is wrong. The Senate was built to weight states rather than people, and it was the condition on which the small states ratified anything at all. The question worth asking is narrower.
