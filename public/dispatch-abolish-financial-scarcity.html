The Attempt to Abolish Financial Scarcity

The treatise argued that a government cannot abolish the scarcity of money by political means, only move it somewhere less convenient. It named where: the long end of the curve, the currency, and the goods people buy on credit. Fourteen months later the ten-year auctioned at the highest yield since the financial crisis and subprime auto delinquency hit a thirty-two year record. The prediction held. The framework’s own instrument did not see it coming, and that failure is the most useful thing in this dispatch.

Scores Section VIII of the treatise, the worked example of the 2025 to 2026 configuration, against the record as of August 2026. Extends the June reading and the outlook to 2029. Conditional, falsifiable, not investment advice.

The claim was simple enough to be checked. An administration was pulling every lever at once: a sweeping tax cut, refunds channelled into household hands, a tariff regime assembled from emergency authority, and a central bank pressed hard enough that a sitting governor faced removal and the outgoing chair faced a criminal investigation, ending with a new chair installed on an explicit mandate to cut rates sharply. Read flat, an aggressive pro-growth programme. Read through the framework, an attempt to abolish the scarcity of money by decree. The treatise said that cannot be done. It said the master scarcity can be suppressed at the short end, where a central bank has direct command, and that it will then reappear somewhere the executive does not control: at the long end of the curve, in the currency, and first of all in the goods that are bought with credit rather than cash. Each of those was checkable. Here is what happened.

The long end refused

In August 2026 the Treasury sold ten-year notes at 4.683 percent, the highest auction yield since the global financial crisis. The ten-year has been trading around 4.68 percent, the term premium is elevated and rising, and the reason given by the market is not a mystery requiring interpretation: inflation running above the central bank’s target and widening budget deficits, with investors demanding more compensation to finance the government. That is the relocation, stated by the people doing the relocating. The short end was commanded. The long end was not available to command, so the scarcity moved there and presented its bill.

This is the part of Section VIII that has aged best, and it is worth being precise about why it counts as a test rather than a description. The framework did not predict that yields would be high, which is a coin flip. It predicted a specific mechanism: that suppression at the short end would produce compensating pressure at the long end, and that the pressure would be attributed to fiscal fear rather than to growth expectations. Both halves are observable and both are what happened.

The manager was not believed

The second prediction was subtler and, to the framework, more important. Section VIII argued that the credibility of the lender of last resort is a reserve accumulated over decades and spent down whenever the institution is bent to fiscal convenience, and that the spending down is priced immediately rather than at the moment of crisis. The test was whether installing a compliant chair would actually deliver easy money.

It did not. Going into the second half of 2026 the Federal Reserve appears likely to stay patient, with inflation sticky, and market expectations never delivered the boom the appointment was meant to produce. The chair changed and the bond market’s scepticism did not. That is the clearest available evidence for the proposition that credibility is a stock rather than a switch, and that an institution which can be ordered to ease is for that reason less able to ease.

A central bank that can be told what to do has less power, not more, because the market discounts the instruction rather than the institution. The easing was announced and pre-discounted in the same quarter.

The canaries, in the order predicted

Section IX said the descent shows up first in credit-coupled goods, the durable purchases whose demand is a demand for credit as much as for the thing itself, and that necessities bought weekly with cash would be last. Housing and automobiles were named specifically. Both are now visibly strained, and the strain has the shape the framework describes.

Subprime auto loans sixty or more days delinquent reached 6.9 percent in January 2026, a thirty-two year record in a series that begins in 1994, and a level above anything reached during the Great Recession. Total auto loan balances stand at $1.69 trillion, with 4.8 percent of all outstanding consumer debt in some stage of delinquency, and the average payment on a new car has passed $770 a month. Existing home sales fell 4.2 percent across the first half of 2026 to an annualised 4.09 million, still well below pre-pandemic levels, with roughly 80 percent of outstanding mortgages carrying a rate at or below 6 percent against a market rate above 6.3 percent. The houses exist. The would-be buyers exist. What is missing is the credit that would move one to the other, which is the counterfeit in its textbook form.

The finding that forces a correction

Here is where this dispatch stops being a victory lap. The framework’s own instrument, the Credit Counterfeit Index, currently reads minus 1.31. That is deeply below its breach threshold and among the more negative readings in the twenty-year history. On its own account the gauge is saying there is no credit counterfeit at all, that monetary conditions are loose relative to the real bind, and that nothing here should be manufacturing scarcity.

Meanwhile subprime auto delinquency sits at a thirty-two year high. Both of those things are true at the same time, and the framework has to account for it rather than pick whichever supports the argument.

The resolution is in the detail of the delinquency data, and it is the same detail that makes it interesting. Prime segments remain stable, with only modest deterioration in bank and captive portfolios. Subprime portfolios are at cycle highs. The stress is not spread across borrowers. It is concentrated at one end of the credit distribution and largely absent at the other.

So the gauge is not wrong so much as incomplete, and the incompleteness is structural. The Credit Counterfeit Index is built from aggregates: an economy-wide credit spread, an economy-wide survey of lending standards, a single policy rate, a single money stock. Aggregates are dominated by the large and the creditworthy. A measure constructed that way will read loose whenever conditions are loose for the borrowers who account for most of the borrowing, and it will keep reading loose while the bottom of the distribution is strangled, because the bottom of the distribution is small in dollar terms and invisible in an average.

That is a real defect and the framework should own it. It also happens to be the same defect the profits and failures chart ran into from a different direction, where corporate profits reached a postwar record while the environment for small firms deteriorated, and neither tax rates nor any aggregate explained the divergence. Two independent lines of evidence now point at the same missing dimension. The treatise’s Section IX already contains the correct idea, that goods sit at different depths in the credit gradient. What neither the treatise nor the gauge does is apply that gradient to borrowers rather than to goods.

The descent does not arrive everywhere at once. It arrives at the bottom of the credit distribution first, and an instrument built on averages is guaranteed to miss it, because at that stage the average is still fine.

The correction, stated plainly so it can be held against the framework later: the Credit Counterfeit Index measures whether the monetary ordering is manufacturing scarcity on average. It cannot tell you for whom. A version that could would need a distributional input, subprime spreads against prime spreads, or small-firm credit availability against large-firm issuance. Until that exists, a deeply negative reading should be understood as saying that no economy-wide counterfeit is under way, and as saying nothing whatever about the units at the bottom, which are precisely the units the descent reaches first.

What this does and does not establish

It does not establish that the administration caused the strain. Subprime auto credit was deteriorating before any of this, the mortgage lock-in is an artefact of the 2021 rate environment rather than of current policy, and the deficit trajectory long predates the current occupant. A framework that claimed all of this as its own would be doing the thing it accuses others of.

What it establishes is narrower and, for a theory, more valuable. The framework named in advance where the pressure would emerge if the attempt were made, and the pressure emerged in those places rather than in others. It did not emerge as consumer price inflation running away, which is where the conventional critique of loose fiscal policy expected it. It did not emerge as a currency collapse. It emerged at the long end of the curve and at the bottom of the credit distribution, which are the two places the ordering says are least defended and most exposed. That is the shape of the claim, and the shape is what a framework is for.

And the correction matters more than the confirmation. Three of the treatise’s predictions held, which is pleasant and proves less than it appears, since a theory that only reports agreement is not being tested. The finding worth carrying forward is that the instrument built to measure this framework cannot see the thing the framework says happens first. That is a real defect, discovered by the evidence, and it is now written down where it can be checked against later.

The factories stand. The houses stand, in numbers, with buyers who want them. The cars are on the lots. Nothing has run out. What is scarce is the claim that moves them, and the attempt to legislate that scarcity away did not abolish it. It moved it to the people least able to argue with it.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
