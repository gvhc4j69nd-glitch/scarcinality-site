The Ceiling That Isn’t

The Toilet Paper Theory says a household at its limit absorbs a squeeze by buying less, and that the buying less is invisible because a purchase that does not happen leaves no record. Housing breaks that rule, and the way it breaks it is the most useful thing the theory has produced. Nobody buys two thirds of a dwelling. When the good cannot be subdivided, the household cannot give way in volume, so the price gives way instead, upward, until it has taken whatever the household has.

An extension to the Toilet Paper Theory rather than an application of it, and the second of its two ceilings after the hours ceiling. The claim is that divisibility, not necessity, decides whether a squeezed household gives way in quantity or in price, and that this single property explains why the cheapest goods and the dearest shelter are the same mechanism seen twice. Conditional, falsifiable, not policy advice.

The original theory rested on a physical bound. A wealthy household does not use four times the toilet paper, so income moved upward stops buying ordinary goods, and a household at the bottom that cannot pay simply consumes less. Both halves of that are right, and both halves quietly assume something the theory never stated: that the good can be bought in smaller amounts. Toilet paper can. Rent cannot. And once that assumption is made explicit, the theory splits cleanly into cases that behave in opposite directions while running on identical logic.

Divisibility is the hinge

Take a household whose income falls by a tenth and ask what it does about each necessity in turn. The answer is not determined by how badly the thing is needed. It is determined by whether the thing comes in pieces.

Divisible necessities Food, paper goods, clothing, fuel for the car. The household buys the cheaper brand, then the smaller package, then fewer of them. Quantity is a continuous dial and the household turns it down. Gives way in volume. Price stays near cost. Nothing is recorded.
Indivisible necessities Shelter above all. There is no two-thirds of a dwelling to buy. The available margins are worse condition, worse location, more people per room, or nothing at all, and each of those is a large discrete step rather than a small adjustment. Gives way in price. Volume holds. The household pays a rising share until it cannot.
Floored necessities Electricity, heat, water. Divisible down to a point and then not at all, because below the floor is a cold house and spoiled food rather than a smaller quantity of warmth. Gives way in volume briefly, then in arrears, then in disconnection.
Mandated necessities Emergency care, examined in the previous dispatch. The law requires delivery regardless of ability to pay, so consumption happens whether the household can finance it or not. Gives way in neither. Converts directly into debt.

This is why the Consumer Expenditure Survey shows what it shows. The bottom quintile spends 41.6 percent of everything it spends on housing, against 29.3 percent in the top quintile. That gap is not evidence that poor households consume more shelter. They consume dramatically less of it, in smaller and worse units. It is evidence that the one necessity they cannot ration is the one that takes the largest bite.

The purest counterfeit on the board

Now apply the treatise’s only test, and housing gives the cleanest answer of any case this framework has examined.

Nothing has run out. There are roughly five empty rental units on the open market for every homeless person in the country, and about fifteen vacant units of every kind. The buildings stand, the plumbing works, the keys exist and are held by people who would prefer them to be earning. No drought, no fire, no material shortage, no strike. The good is present in surplus and the claim on it is absent, which is the treatise’s definition of a counterfeit scarcity stated without any need for interpretation.

Nobody would say the country has run out of housing while ten million homes sit empty. But we say there is a housing shortage constantly, and mean it, because the word shortage has been quietly redefined to mean a shortage of the money to claim one.

The framework’s usual caution applies less than usual here. In the munitions case a bid met a bounded thing and produced a queue, so the scarcity was genuine. In housing the bid meets an existing surplus, and what it produces is a price.

What the squeeze produces instead

Because the volume margin is closed, the pressure has to surface somewhere else, and the numbers are the record of where.

A record 22.7 million renter households, 49 percent of all renters, now spend more than 30 percent of income on housing. Some 12.1 million, 26 percent, spend more than half. And 745,652 people are housed nowhere at all. That is the ladder the indivisibility forces: pay more, pay much more, or fall off it. There is no rung between severe burden and homelessness marked slightly less shelter, because that rung does not exist in the physical world.

Compare the signature to the divisible case and the contrast is the whole argument. When money is tight, toilet paper volumes fall and the price of toilet paper does not move much, because the seller has no leverage over a buyer who can simply purchase fewer rolls. When money is tight, rents keep rising and the quantity of housing consumed barely moves, because the seller is negotiating with a buyer who cannot purchase fewer rooms and cannot leave without ceasing to be housed.

Divisible good, squeezed household:
  quantity falls  →  seller loses volume  →  price disciplined by exit

Indivisible good, squeezed household:
  quantity cannot fall  →  no exit  →  price rises to the household’s limit

Same theory. Same ceiling. Opposite direction,
because one of them comes in pieces and the other does not.

The third case, arriving now

Electricity belongs in the floored category and is worth naming separately, because it is being pushed hard at this exact moment and by an actor the framework has already examined.

Roughly 21 million American households are behind on their energy payments, with something near four million disconnections possible in a year, while the federal assistance program reaches about 6.7 million households on $4.1 billion, or roughly one in six of those eligible. Into that, the data center build-out is adding demand at a scale that moved the PJM capacity price from $28.92 per megawatt-day to $329.17, with data centres responsible for about 63 percent of one auction’s increase.

A household cannot respond to that by consuming a third less heat in January. It responds by consuming slightly less, then by falling behind, then by being disconnected. Arrears are the floored good’s version of going without, and like every other version this framework has found, they are invisible in the aggregate because a household in arrears is still consuming and still counted.

Why this is the sharper test

The Toilet Paper Theory made a prediction that is hard to falsify quickly: that concentrating income upward suppresses volume in ordinary goods. Volume data is slow, noisy, and confounded by a dozen other things.

The extension makes a prediction that is much easier to break. It says the two classes of necessity should behave in opposite directions at the same moment, in the same economy, for the same households. If the theory is right, a period of rising concentration produces flat or falling real prices in divisible staples alongside rising real prices in shelter, and the household budget share of the indivisible good rises while its physical consumption does not. If instead both classes move together, the mechanism proposed here is wrong and the common cause is something else entirely.

That is a test that can be run on published data, this year, without waiting for a cycle.

What it implies, briefly

The framework does not make policy, but it does constrain what a remedy would have to do, and the constraint here is unusually specific.

If shelter gave way in volume, subsidising demand would help, because the household would buy back the quantity it had given up. Because shelter gives way in price, a subsidy paid to a household bidding for an indivisible good in fixed supply is substantially captured by the price. That is not an argument against housing assistance, which keeps people housed today and is the difference between the second rung and the third. It is an argument that assistance alone cannot resolve the condition, because it operates on the side of the equation that is not stuck.

The counterfeit is in the claim, but the reason the claim binds so hard is that the good underneath it cannot be divided. Fix the money and you change who gets the ten million empty units. Fix neither and the price simply keeps climbing until it finds the limit of what a household can bear, which the record tells us is somewhere past half of everything they earn.

Three caveats. Vacancy and homelessness are not directly commensurable: many vacant units are in the wrong places, some are uninhabitable, some are between tenants for a fortnight, and a point-in-time homeless count undercounts the people who are doubled up rather than on a street. The ratio in the chart is an argument about magnitude, not a claim that ten million people could move in tomorrow. Second, housing supply is genuinely constrained in specific metropolitan areas by land, zoning and construction capacity, and in those places the framework’s answer is more mixed than the national aggregate suggests; the third ledger row is written to catch exactly that. Third, the divisibility argument is a mechanism, not a complete account of rent, which also responds to interest rates, migration, household formation and the concentration of ownership.

What survives is the shape. The Toilet Paper Theory found a ceiling on how much of a necessity a person can use, and concluded that moving income upward destroys demand for ordinary things. This dispatch adds that the conclusion only holds where the thing can be bought in smaller pieces. Where it cannot, the same ceiling produces the opposite surface: not demand destroyed but price extracted, not empty shelves but full ones nobody can reach, not a quiet reduction in volume but a household paying more than half of everything it earns for a roof, in a country with ten million empty ones.

Scarcinality: a theory of which shortage is real.
