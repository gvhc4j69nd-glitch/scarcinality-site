The Years Nobody Counted

Ask which president made American flying safe and the federal record cannot answer you. Not because the question is unfair. Because the twenty six years holding the answer are missing from both official files. What is recorded to the individual vote is the money.

Built from the two accident census files the NTSB publishes for download, counted on 15 November 2026, and from roll call votes at the Clerk of the House and the Senate. Three claims: the official record has a twenty six year hole, the safety trend inside the parts we can see shows no party signal, and the input now being drawn down is controller time rather than money. Conditional, falsifiable, and limited by what the files contain.

The National Transportation Safety Board publishes two files. One is named PRE1982. The other is named avall. Together they are the record of American civil aviation accidents, and the agency describes them as covering 1962 to the present.

They are about 136 megabytes. Download both and count what is inside.

The first file holds 87,038 events. The last one is in December 1981.

The second holds 31,773 events. The first one is in January 2008.

There is nothing in between.

Twenty six years are not thinned or incomplete. They are absent. The NTSB's own download page says avall covers 1982 to the present, and the file dated 1 November 2026 begins in 2008.

Four presidents sat inside that gap. The hole is not at the edge of the record, where holes are forgivable. It is in the middle, where the answer is.

What the files do say

Assign every event to the president in office that day. Then count only the days the files actually cover.

Four presidencies are covered end to end in the early file. Johnson, Nixon, Ford, Carter. Four more are covered end to end in the modern one. Obama, Trump, Biden, and Trump again.

Between those two groups there is a void, and the void has names in it.

Look at the first group. Two Democrats, two Republicans, alternating. The numbers run 1,523, then 1,649, then 1,607, then 1,986.

Now the second group. Two Democrats, two Republicans, alternating again. The numbers run 422, then 362, then 325, then 338.

The spread inside each group is about thirty percent. The gap between the groups is a factor of four.

Party does not predict the height of a single bar. The era does, completely.

The improvement is inside the hole

Something reduced American aviation deaths by roughly three quarters. It did not happen in the 1960s or the 1970s, because those years are flat. It did not happen after 2008, because those years are flat too.

It happened between 1981 and 2008. That is the window neither file covers.

The hole is not uniform, and the distinction matters. The NTSB does publish annual statistical tables, and the ones for airlines and general aviation run from 1992 through 2011. So aggregate counts exist for part of the gap. What does not exist, in the files the agency tells you to download, is the underlying events.

For 1982 through 1991 even the aggregate tables run out.

Count the damage in days. Since Kennedy's inauguration there have been 24,040 days of presidential time. The two files cover 14,180 of them.

Forty one percent of the period has no entry.

What is recorded perfectly

Now set the accidents aside and look at the money. Here the record is immaculate. Every vote, every member, every date.

Read the top of that table and the bottom as two different activities.

Authorizing the FAA is almost unanimous. In 2024 not one Republican senator voted against it. Only 2012 broke along party lines, and the dispute there was a union election rule, not aviation.

Funding it is where the machine fails. And the failures are getting longer.

Ten controllers

The 2018 and 2019 shutdown ran thirty four days by the Congressional Research Service's count. It closed seven appropriations bills, including the one that pays for the FAA.

Air traffic controllers kept working. They are what the law calls excepted, meaning they stay on the job and their pay stops.

On Friday 25 January 2019, controllers called in sick at the Washington and Jacksonville centers. Those two facilities handle the high altitude airspace along the East Coast. Departures backed up at LaGuardia, Newark and Philadelphia.

A three week stopgap was enacted later that day.

CRS, surveying how the standoff broke, cites a CNN account with a number in its title. Ten controllers stayed home.

Two chambers had just voted down each other's bills with sixty vote thresholds and five weeks of position taking. What ended it was ten people on one shift.

It happened again, larger. The shutdown that began on 1 October 2025 ran forty three days, the longest government wide shutdown in American history. On 6 October the tower at Hollywood Burbank went unstaffed for an afternoon and evening, because nobody assigned to the shift came in. The airport stayed open and aircraft separated themselves by procedure.

On 7 November the FAA ordered airlines to cut flights at forty high impact airports. Four percent, stepping to six on 11 November, eight on 13 November, ten on 14 November.

The shutdown ended on 12 November. The flight restrictions did not come off until 19 November.

The input that is actually running out

Twenty three lapses in appropriations have occurred since fiscal 1977. The four most recent are the ones that reached the airspace.

Here is what has been happening to controller time underneath all of it.

Overtime more than tripled. Productive time per shift fell by forty minutes. The FAA's own language is that chronic overtime leads to fatigue, burnout and loss of retention.

That is a stock being drawn down, not a flow being managed.

Money is not the scarce thing

This is the part that should be strange. The authorization is not in doubt. The 2024 act runs through fiscal 2028 and passed the Senate without a single Republican no.

Nor is the cash missing. The Airport and Airway Trust Fund took in more than $21 billion in fiscal 2026 by CBO's estimate. It ended the year with a balance above $23 billion.

More than $10 billion of that was uncommitted.

So there is money in a dedicated fund, and a statute authorizing its use, and controllers working unpaid anyway. The tax authority continues during a lapse. Airlines keep collecting and remitting. What stops is the annual appropriation, and without that nothing in the fund can be spent.

The bind is not the money and it is not the law. It is the one mechanism that has to clear every twelve months, and it is the mechanism that has failed twenty three times.

Read against the treatise, this is the ordinary shape of a real constraint hiding behind a nominal abundance. The balance sheet says the resource is plentiful. The thing that is actually short is the margin in a few thousand people's schedules, and no account reports it.

The target moved instead

On 15 May 2026 the FAA published a new workforce plan. It set full staffing at 12,563 certified controllers.

The previous target, developed in 2023 with the controllers' union, was 14,633.

The requirement fell by 2,070 positions. The FAA attributes the revision to a National Academy of Sciences review of its staffing models. It pairs the new target with automated scheduling meant to cut mandatory overtime. The union says it was not involved in writing the plan.

Both things can be true. A 2023 model can have been wrong, and a gap can also be closed on paper by moving the line you are measuring against.

What is checkable is the arithmetic. Against the old target the shortfall was roughly 3,600. Against the new one it is roughly 1,600. Staffing moved by a few hundred. The target moved by two thousand.

Where this stands today

The government is open. Fiscal 2027 is running on a stopgap, H.R. 6500, which the Senate passed 90 to 6 on 8 August 2026. Republicans 47 to 2. Democrats 42 to 3.

Nearly unanimous, again, and temporary, again.

It expires on 11 December 2026. That is twenty six days from this writing. The Senate has not adopted a single one of its twelve regular fiscal 2027 appropriations bills.

The case against this reading

Three objections, and the first is serious.

The fatality counts here are not normalized by exposure. General aviation flying hours fell substantially after the 1970s, and general aviation is where most of these deaths are. So part of the four fold decline is fewer small aircraft flying, not safer flying. The direction survives, because airline traffic grew a great deal over the same period. But the magnitude in the figure is not a pure safety improvement, and should not be read as one.

Second, airline fatalities are too rare to compare across four year terms. Biden's four years recorded zero deaths on United States airlines in the file. Trump's second term records seventy, almost all from one midair collision in January 2025. One event decides the ranking. Any story built on that comparison is a story about a single Wednesday.

Third, the lower staffing target may simply be right. If the 2023 model overstated the requirement, then correcting it is good administration rather than an evasion, and better scheduling tools really can reduce the need for bodies. That is a testable claim, which is the next section.

What would prove this wrong

Four things, in descending order of sharpness.

If the FAA reaches 12,563 certified controllers while overtime use falls back toward three percent, the staffing argument here is wrong. The target will have been a correction and not a climb down. Watch both numbers together, because either one alone can be met the wrong way.

Say the 11 December deadline passes with regular appropriations enacted, and no further stopgap. Then the mechanism is less broken than this dispatch claims.

If the NTSB publishes a census file covering 1982 through 2007, the central finding expires. That would be the best possible outcome for the argument and the worst for the piece.

And say a future shutdown produces no operational effect. No callouts, no flight reductions, no unstaffed towers. Then controller margin is not the binding constraint, and something else is.

Two cautions on the data. The online CAROL search states that aviation records run from 1962 to the present. It may well serve the missing years through its web interface. Its query API refused requests from this environment, so that could not be tested directly. The claim here is narrower. It is about the two bulk files, which is what researchers and reporters actually download.

Also, the modern file is an accident census and includes a small number of investigated incidents, 2,378 of 31,453 classified events. The early file is coded differently. Comparisons across the two are directionally sound and not precise to the unit.
