Aid, Migration, and the Money That Goes Home

Three things are usually argued in three separate rooms: what America spends on aid to poor countries, who migrates from those countries, and what that migration costs or contributes once here. Put the three flows on one page and the striking part is not any of them individually. It is that the largest by a wide margin is the one no legislature ever voted on.

This page reports numbers and the disagreements between them. It does not recommend an immigration policy or an aid policy, and where the research genuinely conflicts, both sides are shown and the reader is handed the switch.

One. What America sends

Foreign aid occupies about one percent of the federal budget, a figure most Americans put many times higher when surveyed. In 2026 it also collapsed. Congress appropriated roughly $50 billion for the fiscal year, itself 16 percent below 2025, but USAID had been shut down, nearly all of its ten thousand staff terminated along with as many as 280,000 contracted workers, and its surviving programs moved to the State Department and Agriculture. As of 1 July, actual disbursements for the fiscal year stood just under $7 billion.

Two of those rows are corridor-specific and two are total inflows, which is a real inconsistency in the underlying sources rather than a rounding choice. It matters little for the comparison, because roughly nine in ten remittance dollars reaching Guatemala and Honduras come from the United States, but it is worth knowing which row is which.

Remittances to Guatemala alone run about forty times the entire annual budget once requested for the Central America root-causes strategy, the flagship American program for reducing the pressure to migrate.

Globally the same shape holds. Remittances to low- and middle-income countries reached roughly $685 billion in 2025 against about $185 billion of official development assistance from all donors combined, a ratio near 3.7 to 1, and they arrive as household income rather than as programs.

Three. What it costs, and earns, at home

Here the evidence is genuinely contested, and the contest turns on an accounting choice most summaries never mention. The National Academies found that the sign of the long-run fiscal effect depends on how you assign the cost of public goods such as national defense and interest on the debt.

If each immigrant is charged an average share of those costs, immigration looks fiscally negative in both the short and long run. If they are charged the marginal cost, which is close to nothing, because another resident does not require another aircraft carrier, the long-run effect is positive. Same data, same researchers, opposite answer.

Immigrants’ children have a more favorable net fiscal impact than either the first or the third-plus generation.

The generational split The first generation is on average more costly to government than the native-born, and the cost falls mainly on states and localities, because that is who runs schools and emergency rooms. The second generation is among the strongest fiscal contributors in the entire population. Whether immigration is a cost therefore depends heavily on where you stop the clock.
The level-of-government split This is the part that explains why the argument is loudest locally. The costs are concentrated at state and local level and arrive immediately. The revenues are concentrated at federal level and arrive over decades. A school district and the Treasury are looking at the same person and correctly reaching opposite conclusions.
The horizon split CBO scored the recent surge over ten years and found revenues exceeding outlays by $0.9 trillion. A one-year or three-year window on the same population would show the opposite, because settlement costs land before earnings do.

What the three panels add up to

Read together rather than separately, the flows describe something the separate arguments miss. American aid to poor countries is small, has just been cut hard, and is frequently justified as reducing the pressure to migrate. Migration from those same countries produces a private transfer back to them that is an order of magnitude larger than the aid, is spent by households rather than administered by agencies, and rises when the receiving country is in trouble rather than falling. And the fiscal effect of that migration inside the United States is negative or positive depending on an accounting convention, a generation, and a time horizon, which is why two honest people can read the same National Academies report and quote opposite conclusions.

None of that settles what policy should be. It does suggest that the three questions are one question, and that answering any of them while ignoring the other two produces a number that cannot be right.

Sources and limits

Aid figures: congressional appropriations for FY2026 and disbursement tracking through 1 July 2026. Remittances: World Bank and KNOMAD migration and development data, and central bank reporting for Guatemala and Honduras. Migration: DHS Office of Homeland Security Statistics, where Mexico remained the leading country of birth for both new and resident lawful permanent residents. Fiscal estimates: Congressional Budget Office, Effects of the Immigration Surge on the Federal Budget and the Economy, 2024; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, The Economic and Fiscal Consequences of Immigration, 2016.

Four limits. Remittance data captures formal transfers and misses cash carried by hand, so the true flow is larger than shown. The corridor and total-inflow rows are not perfectly comparable, as noted above. Fiscal estimates depend on assumptions that advocacy organizations on both sides select to favour their conclusion, which is why only CBO and the National Academies are used here and why the assumption that flips the sign has been made into a switch rather than a footnote. And aid, migration and remittances influence each other in ways no table settles: the causal question of whether aid reduces migration remains genuinely open in the research literature.
