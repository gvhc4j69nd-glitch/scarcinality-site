What the Farm Payment Covers

EIA · farmdoc daily · USDA FSA · USGS · 2026

The corn line is almost a coincidence. Add up what an acre of corn costs more this year and you get about $42. The Farmer Bridge Assistance payment for corn was $44.36 an acre. The check covers the cost increase and leaves about two dollars over.

Then look at soybeans, where the same arithmetic gives about $12 of extra cost against a $30.88 payment. Two and a half times. If these payments were compensating input costs, corn would get the larger one, because corn is where the costs went up.

They are not compensating inputs. They are compensating the other side of the ledger: the export market, which is why soybeans, the crop that lost the most sales abroad, gets the better rate. That is a defensible thing to do. It is just not the thing the payment is usually described as doing, and the per-acre numbers make the difference visible.

Potash is the surprise. The common assumption is that the trade policy raised fertilizer prices, and for potash specifically that is not what happened. Canada supplies 79 percent of American potash, and fertilizer has been carved out of every round of Canadian tariffs this year, including the 50 percent round in July. Only potash that fails the CUSMA origin test pays anything, at 10 percent. Corn Belt retail potash went from $493 to $505 a ton across the spring. On a normal corn application that is about 80 cents an acre.

The global benchmark did move much harder, from about $358 a metric ton in January to $488 in March, its highest since early 2023. Very little of that reached the Corn Belt, where the retail price was already high. Two potash prices, two different stories, and the one farmers actually pay barely budged.

Diesel is the whole shock, and diesel is not a trade story either. It went from $3.68 a gallon to $6.22, up 69 percent in a year, with a record $6.53 set the week of 21 September. That is the oil disruption, the same one driving the freight numbers and the index reading elsewhere on this site. On 19 October the administration announced a further $8 billion Harvest Fuel Relief Program, which names the problem correctly in its title. Per-crop rates for it were still pending in November.

The totals. Government payments to farmers in 2026 are on course for about $55.4 billion, against $45.6 billion in 2020, which had been the record. Roughly $12 billion of this year's total had already gone out under Bridge Assistance before the fuel program was announced.

The scarcinality reading. This page sits at the join between the two kinds of shortage. Nitrogen and potash are monetary problems: the plants exist, the ore exists, and what moved was price. Diesel in 2026 is closer to a real one, because the constraint is physical and sits in the oil market rather than in a ledger. That distinction decides what a payment can do. A check written against a monetary shortage relieves it. A check written against a real shortage does not produce any diesel, it just gives the holder a stronger bid for the diesel that exists. Eight billion dollars of fuel relief, spread across a national fuel market that is genuinely tight, is a transfer rather than a remedy. That is not an argument against sending it. It is an argument for knowing which one you are doing.

How much to trust these numbers. The per-acre figures are budget models, not receipts. Diesel use swings enormously with tillage: a conventional corn system runs about 5 gallons an acre before harvest and hauling, and a no-till system runs about 2, so the diesel bar here could reasonably be half or double what is shown. Fertilizer costs come from the Illinois budgets and a farm in Georgia or North Dakota will not match them. The payment rates are national and uniform, which is precisely why they fit nobody exactly. And the comparison deliberately leaves out the output side, where the larger damage sits and where the numbers are far harder to pin down. Arguments welcome. Read: Cheap and Heavy →
