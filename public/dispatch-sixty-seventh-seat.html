The Sixty-Seventh Seat

Democrats won both chambers on 3 November. Overturning a veto takes 67 senators, and the 67th seat on the 2028 map is Louisiana, which they lost this month by under two points. Why a veto-proof Senate almost never arrives when it would be needed, and what a Congress built around vetoed bills does to the voters in each seat up in 2028.

A reading on the 2028 Senate map and the arithmetic of overriding a president, built from the Senate's veto records, the House Historian's party divisions, the 2024 presidential results by state, Senate roll calls from 2015 and 2017, and the 2026 returns as reported through 18 November. Four claims. The 2028 election cannot produce a Congress that overrides Trump, whose term ends seventeen days after it sits. Sixty Senate seats are inside a repeat of 2026, and 67 are outside it. Override votes tell a state's voters far more than passage votes do. And a vetoed bill is a promissory note that the next election pays or tears up. One caveat up front. The ranking uses 2024 presidential margins, which order states well and predict single races poorly. Conditional, falsifiable, not a forecast.

On 19 October 1974, Gerald Ford stood in front of a crowd in Greenville, South Carolina, and asked it to fear a phrase. The phrase was veto-proof Congress. He said it six times in one speech.

“I don't think we want a veto-proof Congress which would be a legislative dictatorship,” he said.

Seventeen days later the voters gave him one, or what looked like one. Democrats won 291 seats in the House. Two thirds of 435 is 290.

Here is what happened next. In that Congress Ford vetoed 32 bills outright. Congress overrode eight. It tried and failed twelve times. Six of the failures came in the House, the chamber with the supermajority.

The first big test came on 10 June 1975. The bill regulated strip mining. The override got 278 votes. It needed 281.

A party held 291 seats and could not find 281 votes. That gap is what this dispatch is about.

Where things actually stand

Start with the premise, because part of it is not true yet.

Democrats did win both chambers on 3 November. The Senate will sit 56 to 44, counting two independents who caucus with Democrats. Nine Republican seats flipped. They run from Maine to Alaska and include Texas, Kansas, and Mississippi. In the House, Democrats hold at least 257 seats, with seven races still uncalled. Their popular vote margin was about fourteen points, the largest in a midterm since 1974.

But nobody controls the new Congress until it convenes on 3 January 2027. Until then, Republicans run both chambers.

The second correction matters more. The 2028 election chooses the Congress that sits from 3 January 2029. Donald Trump's term ends at noon on 20 January 2029, and the Twenty-Second Amendment bars a third. The Congress elected in 2028 overlaps Trump by seventeen days. No supermajority won in 2028 will override him.

Whatever overriding gets done against Trump will be done by the Congress already elected. The 2028 supermajority is aimed at whoever comes next. That turns out to change everything.

Three numbers in each chamber

A majority passes a bill. In the House that is 218. In the Senate it is 51, or 50 and the vice president.

Ending a filibuster takes three fifths of the Senate. That is 60.

Overriding a veto takes two thirds of each chamber. That is 67 senators and 290 representatives, if everyone votes. The Supreme Court read the rule in 1919 as two thirds of those present and voting. Absences lower the bar. They lower it for both sides.

So a supermajority that overcomes any Republican challenge means two different things. With a Democrat in the White House, there is no veto to override, and the number that matters is 60. With a Republican there, it is 67 and 290.

The next two years

Do the arithmetic for the Congress that actually faces Trump.

In the House, 257 Democrats need about 33 Republicans to reach 290. That is closer than it sounds. On 8 January 2026 the House voted on Trump's veto of a Colorado water pipeline. The bill had cleared both chambers without a recorded objection. Every Democrat and 35 Republicans voted to override. Under the old numbers it failed, 248 to 177. In the new House the same coalition would land right at the line.

The Senate is where it stops. Fifty-six Democrats need eleven Republicans.

In June the Senate voted to end the Iran campaign. It was the most contested question of the year. Four Republicans voted with the Democrats: Susan Collins, Bill Cassidy, Lisa Murkowski, and Rand Paul. Cassidy lost his primary in May. Collins lost her seat this month. Of the four, two remain.

Eleven is almost three times the number that crossed on the hardest vote of the year. Trump has vetoed two bills since January 2025. Neither was overridden. Across the whole history of the country, presidents have vetoed 2,599 bills. Congress overrode 112. About four in a hundred.

Which bills get overridden is the more useful fact.

What gets overridden

The last time a Democratic Congress faced a Republican president was 2007 and 2008. George W. Bush vetoed eleven bills. Four were overridden.

Two were the farm bill, passed twice because of a clerical error. One funded water projects. One stopped a cut to what Medicare pays doctors. The House override votes ran 316 to 108, 317 to 109, 361 to 54, and 383 to 41.

These were not Democratic bills. Members of both parties had written them for their own districts.

Everything else stood. A timeline for leaving Iraq. Stem cell research. A health and labor spending bill. An intelligence bill. And the children's health insurance expansion, twice. The first of those overrides got 273 votes in the House. It needed 286.

A veto is overridden when the president's own party wanted the bill. It holds when the bill was written to make a point. That is settled before the vote, not during it.

The cheapest vote in Washington

There is a second reason message bills hold. It says something uncomfortable about what voters learn from them.

On 3 December 2015 the Senate voted to repeal most of the Affordable Care Act. It passed, 52 to 47. Everyone knew President Obama would veto it. In January he did.

Nineteen months later, the Senate had a Republican president who would sign it. On 26 July 2017 it voted on a repeal modeled on the 2015 bill. It failed, 45 to 55.

Seven Republicans voted no. Six of them had voted yes in 2015. Lamar Alexander. Shelley Moore Capito. Dean Heller. John McCain. Lisa Murkowski. Rob Portman.

Same senators. A bill built on the same model. Opposite result. The only thing that had changed was whether the vote would count.

That is the trouble with a Congress that sends up bill after bill to be vetoed. A vote on a bill that cannot become law costs nothing, so it tells voters very little. The votes that carry information are the expensive ones.

An override vote is expensive. It is the moment a senator's own voters and the president's coalition pull in opposite directions, and the senator has to choose one.

The bill that comes back

A vetoed bill does one more thing. It is the reason the strategy is not foolish.

Ford's strip mining bill failed by three votes in June 1975. Ford lost the 1976 election. On 3 August 1977, Jimmy Carter signed nearly the same law.

Bush's children's health bill was vetoed twice in 2007. His party lost the 2008 election. On 4 February 2009, a little over two weeks into his term, Barack Obama signed the expansion.

A vetoed bill is a promissory note. It names the policy and the price. It is payable only if the next election goes a particular way.

That is the real consequence for the voters in the seats up in 2028. A Congress that spends two years collecting vetoes changes nothing in law. It moves the decision to them.

What Democrats plan to send is already public. Restoring the expired Affordable Care Act subsidies. Reversing the Medicaid cuts in last year's budget law. Blocking the global tariffs. Cutting off funding for the Iran war.

Some of the deadlines arrive before the new Congress does. States must start Medicaid work requirements for adults in the expansion group by 1 January 2027. The new Congress sits two days later. A repeal would meet a veto, and the requirement would stand at least through 2028. Of the nineteen Republican seats up in 2028, thirteen are in states that expanded Medicaid, where the requirement applies.

The map, seat by seat

Now the 2028 map. Thirty-five Senate seats are up. Democrats hold sixteen and Republicans nineteen. That count includes a special election in Tennessee for the seat Marsha Blackburn leaves in January to become governor. Forty Democratic seats are not up.

So Democrats reach 60 by holding all sixteen and winning four more. They reach 67 by holding all sixteen and winning eleven of the nineteen.

Rank the 35 states by how they voted for president in 2024. Assume Democrats win every seat up to some point and none beyond it. That is generous in a specific way. It hands them every easier seat for free.

Read it from the left. The eleven seats in states Kamala Harris carried bring Democrats to 51. Then the map turns. To go further, they must hold five seats in states Trump won: Pennsylvania, Georgia, Nevada, Arizona, and Ohio. And they must take Wisconsin and North Carolina.

The 60th seat is a three-way tie. Florida, Alaska, and Iowa all voted for Trump by about thirteen points, within a tenth of a point of one another.

The 67th seat is Louisiana. Trump carried it by 22. After Louisiana the map falls off a cliff. The next seat is South Dakota, at 29.

The dashed line marks the reddest state where a Democrat won a Senate seat this month. That was Mississippi, at 22.9, by about 4,700 votes. Louisiana sits just under the line. On 3 November Democrats lost Louisiana's other seat by 25,857 votes out of more than 1.46 million.

The 67th seat sits at the exact edge of the biggest wave in fifty years.

What a repeat of 2026 buys

The fair test is to replay this month on the 2028 map.

Take the eighteen 2026 Senate races in states Trump carried. Measure each result against Trump's 2024 margin in the same state. The median swing toward the Democrat was eighteen points. I left out Texas, whose count is not final. On early returns it swung further than any of them.

Now apply eighteen points to every seat on the 2028 map.

Sixty is inside the wave, with room to spare. Sixty-seven is outside it.

Reaching Louisiana takes a swing of 22 points. Three of the eighteen races swung that far: North Carolina, South Carolina, and Mississippi. To get to 67, Democrats would need that in every state from Missouri to Louisiana, and lose nothing behind them.

Why 67 arrives when it is not needed

Here is the part that changes how to think about the whole question.

Two thirds of the Senate only matters if a Republican wins the White House in 2028. A Democratic president does not veto Democratic bills.

But 2028 is a presidential year. In presidential years, states vote for the Senate the way they vote for president. In 2016 and 2020 combined, 68 of 69 Senate races went the same way as the state's presidential vote. In 2024, four states split. All four were swing states.

To win in Louisiana, Utah, Missouri, and Indiana in 2028, a Democratic Senate candidate would need a tide strong enough to carry those states for president, or nearly. In that world the Democrat is in the White House. The veto disappears.

The world where Democrats need 67 is the world where they cannot get it. The world where they could get it is the world where they do not need it.

The record agrees. Since the Civil War, the party opposed to the president has held two thirds of the Senate in two Congresses. Both were the Reconstruction Congresses that fought Andrew Johnson, from 1865 to 1869. The closest since came in 1959, when Democrats held 64 of 98 seats against Eisenhower. In 1975, after Watergate, they held 61.

The only 67 that matters is the one a presidential year will not deliver. The number actually on the ballot in 2028 is 60.

The House needs something it has never done

The House runs into a different wall.

Since 1945, a party has held 290 seats in three Congresses. Democrats had 295 in 1965, 291 in 1975, and 292 in 1977. Never since.

Democrats now have at least 257. Reaching 290 means gaining about 33 more in 2028, after gaining about 42 this year.

Look at what followed every comparable wave. Since 1946 there have been eight midterms in which one party gained 30 or more House seats. In the presidential election that followed, that party lost seats five times. It gained three times: by five in 1968, by one in 1976, and by 24 in 2008.

No party has followed a wave with anything close to 33. A veto-proof House in 2029 would be without precedent.

What the vetoes ask of each seat

Now put the voters back in. A Congress that spends two years sending up bills to be vetoed creates a different problem in each kind of seat.

Five Republicans up in 2028 represent states that elected a Democrat to the Senate this month. Ted Budd in North Carolina. Chuck Grassley in Iowa. Jerry Moran in Kansas. Tim Scott in South Carolina. Lisa Murkowski in Alaska. Their general electorates just moved. Their primary electorates did not. Every override vote in 2027 and 2028 makes them pick one.

For voters in those five states, the override votes are the record worth watching. Passage votes on doomed bills will tell them little. The 2015 repeal showed what those are worth.

Murkowski deserves her own paragraph. She is one of the six who voted for repeal when it was safe and against it when it was real. She was one of the four who voted to end the Iran campaign. Her seat is also the 60th on the map. To reach 60 through Alaska, Democrats would have to defeat the Republican most likely to vote with them already.

A second group sits in Trump states with no Senate race this year. Ron Johnson in Wisconsin. Eric Schmitt in Missouri. Todd Young in Indiana. Mike Lee in Utah. Their voters have not said anything yet. Override votes will be the first evidence either way.

Then come John Kennedy in Louisiana and Ashley Moody in Florida. Both states held for Republicans this month by under three points. After them are the safe seats, from South Dakota to Idaho. There, an override vote costs a senator nothing but a primary.

The pressure runs the other way for five Democrats. John Fetterman, Raphael Warnock, Catherine Cortez Masto, Mark Kelly, and Sherrod Brown all represent states Trump carried. Brown won his seat this month by under five points in a state Trump carried by eleven. Every bill built to be vetoed is a vote the 2028 Republican nominee can run against.

That is the risk Harry Truman showed.

The Truman problem

In 1947 Republicans took both chambers for the first time since 1931. They sent Harry Truman bill after bill. He vetoed many of them. They overrode him on the big one, the Taft-Hartley labor law.

Then Truman ran against them. He called them the do-nothing Congress. He summoned them back for a special session in the summer of 1948 and dared them to pass their own platform. They did not.

In November Truman won. Democrats gained 75 House seats.

The lesson is not that vetoes hurt whoever issues them. It is that a stalemate gets blamed on somebody, and voters choose who. In 1948 the vetoing president was on the ballot and made the case himself. In 2028 Trump will not be on the ballot. His party's nominee inherits the vetoes without the voice. That cuts in Democrats' favor. It is the strongest argument for the strategy.

The case against this reading

Four ways this is wrong, or at least too tidy.

The first is that this month already broke the ranking. Democrats won Mississippi at Trump +22.9 and lost Florida at +13.1. North Carolina swung 23 points with a former governor on the ballot. Florida swung ten. Candidates are not furniture. A map ranked by presidential margin will be wrong in particular states, and it could be wrong in the Democrats' favor.

The second is that I have undersold the free vote. A doomed bill is how a party tells voters what it would do. The children's health expansion became law in 2009 partly because the 2007 vetoes had made it a campaign issue. A Congress that only sends bills the president will sign has let him write its agenda.

The third is that 60 may be enough. With a Democratic president and 60 senators, most of what gets vetoed in 2027 could pass in 2029. Budget measures need only 51 through reconciliation. On that reading, 67 is the wrong target, and the vetoes are an investment in the right one.

The fourth is that override math can be engineered. Bills can be written to attract Republicans. The Colorado pipeline drew 35 House Republicans in January. A Congress that chose bills for their override potential, rather than their message, could win more than the four Bush suffered. That is the 2007 pattern run on purpose.

What would prove this wrong

If the Senate overrides a Trump veto in 2027 or 2028 on a bill he made a priority, the claim that the Senate is the wall fails. A priority means a policy his administration created or a bill he campaigned against, not a local project. Eleven Republicans would have to cross.

Count the override votes. Take the five Republicans up in 2028 in states that elected Democrats this month: Budd, Grassley, Moran, Scott, and Murkowski. If they do not vote to override more often than the other fourteen Republicans up in 2028, the cross-pressure reading is wrong.

If Democrats hold 67 Senate seats after the 2028 election, I was wrong about the map. If they win Louisiana, Utah, Indiana, or Missouri while their presidential nominee loses that state by more than ten points, I was wrong about presidential years.

If Democrats win 290 House seats in 2028, the wave history above stops being a guide.

And the promissory note. Say Democrats win the White House and both chambers in 2028. Then the main bills Trump vetoes in 2027 and 2028 should become law by the end of 2029. If they do not, the vetoes were never notes. They were press releases.

One caution on method. The ranking uses 2024 presidential margins, and the replay applies one median swing to every state. Both are reasonable ways to order a map. Neither forecasts any single race. Tennessee holds its special election in 2028 under its vacancy law, though one local report places it in 2030. Moving it changes nothing that matters here, because Tennessee sits beyond Louisiana either way. Seat totals are as reported through 18 November and may move by one or two as the last counts finish.
