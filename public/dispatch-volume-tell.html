Dispatch · Extending the Toilet Paper Theory · as of 13 September 2026

The Volume Tell

Every theory should make a prediction that could embarrass it. This one did. If a household can only use so much of a necessity, then the companies selling necessities should be making more money selling fewer things. That is checkable every ninety days, and the answer turns out to be more interesting than a yes.

An extension of the Toilet Paper Theory, and the first test of its most falsifiable claim. Three findings: the category data confirms the prediction, the manufacturers’ own reports appear to refute it, and the resolution of that conflict is the part worth keeping. Conditional, falsifiable, not investment advice.

Nobody buys twice as much toilet paper because they got a raise. That sentence is the whole theory, and it has an awkward consequence that can be checked against quarterly filings.

If the amount of a necessity a household can use is roughly fixed, then the companies selling necessities face a wall. They cannot grow by selling more. Whatever growth they report has to come from charging more for the same amount, or from taking customers off a competitor.

So the tell is this. Look for revenue rising while units fall. If you find it across a whole category, and you find the missing units turning up at the cheaper end of the shelf, the theory is doing real work. If instead volumes grow along with prices, the theory is wrong and should be said to be wrong.

The first half of 2026 is the first clean test. Here is what it says.

The category answers yes

Across American grocery in the first six months of this year, national brands sold 0.5 percent fewer units than a year earlier. Over the same six months, they collected 2.2 percent more dollars.

That gap is the entire prediction in one line. Fewer things left the shelf. More money came in.

And the units did not vanish. They moved. Store brands sold 0.2 percent more units while their dollar sales stayed flat, which is what taking share at a lower price looks like in arithmetic. Store brands are now 23.8 percent of every unit sold in an American grocery store. That is the highest share ever recorded.

The companies answer no

Now the complication, which needs to be put on the table rather than left out.

Go to the filings of the companies themselves and the picture does not look like the chart above. Procter and Gamble reported organic sales up 3 percent in the January to March quarter, and 2 points of that came from volume. Kimberly-Clark, which actually makes the toilet paper, reported something stranger still. Volume-led growth of 4.1 percent. And price investments of negative 1.5 percent.

Read that plainly. The largest tissue maker in the country grew by selling more, and it did that by charging less.

Taken at face value, that is the opposite of the prediction. A theory that says necessity firms must grow on price has just been handed two firms growing on volume.

A prediction that only counts the quarters it wins is not a prediction. So here is the honest position: at the level of the individual manufacturer, in this quarter, the simple version of the tell failed.

Two things are worth saying before rescuing anything. These are global companies, and their reported figures blend dozens of countries with an American grocery aisle. And organic sales is a measure built for investors, not for this argument. Neither of those is a refutation, but neither is an excuse either.

Which one is wrong

The two readings cannot both be describing the same thing, so look at what the phrase price investment actually means.

It means cutting the price. A company does not describe a price cut as an investment when demand is strong. It describes it that way when it has lost units and wants them back.

So ask why Kimberly-Clark would give up 1.5 points of price to buy 4.1 points of volume. If households could simply be sold more tissue, the company would raise price and sell more at once, the way a firm behaves in a growing market. It did not do that. It traded margin for units, which is the behavior of a company operating against a fixed quantity of demand that somebody else had taken.

That is the ceiling asserting itself from the other direction. In 2022 it looked like firms taking price because volumes could not grow. In 2026 it looks like firms giving price back because volumes would not return. Both are the same wall seen from opposite sides.

And the category numbers are the arbiter here, because they count the aisle rather than the corporation. In the aisle, national brand units fell.

The part that does not reverse

Here is the finding that changes this from a cyclical story into a structural one.

Ninety-four percent of shoppers say they will keep buying store brands even if grocery prices come down.

Trading down has always been understood as a recession behavior. Money gets tight, people buy the cheaper thing, money loosens, they go back. That rhythm is well documented and it has broken. Private label share climbed through 2024, climbed through 2025, and kept climbing into 2026, through a period nobody would describe as a downturn.

This matters for the theory specifically. A capped-demand argument and an income argument make the same prediction during a squeeze and different predictions afterward. If households were merely short of money, the units would come back when the money did. They are not coming back.

The trade-down is also no longer a poor household’s behavior. Dollar General reports customers trading down at an accelerated rate, and the fastest growth in that behavior is among households earning more than $100,000 a year. A ceiling that only bound the bottom decile would not show up in six-figure households.

The tell beneath the tell

There is one more move available to a company that cannot raise price and cannot lose units, and the toilet paper aisle is where you can see it most clearly.

Shrink the unit.

Quilted Northern took its mega roll from 295 sheets down to 255. Across the category, a mega roll that carried 264 sheets in 2022 now carries 224. And the sheet itself got narrower, from the 4.5 inches that was standard to 3.9 inches on most brands today.

Those two reductions multiply. A roll with 15 percent fewer sheets, each of them 13 percent narrower, holds roughly a quarter less paper than the roll that sat under the same name a few years ago. The price on the shelf may not have moved at all.

Which means the volume figures understate the case. When a unit shrinks by a quarter and still counts as one unit, a flat volume number is a real decline that the measurement cannot see.

This is the theory in its purest form, and it is worth noticing that it happens in the specific product the theory is named after. Demand is fixed at something like a household’s actual need. Price cannot rise without losing the sale. So the package quietly gives you less, and the ceiling gets paid anyway.

Where this leaves the claim

The prediction survives, in a more careful form than it was made.

What the theory got right is the category. Dollars and units have separated, the separation is in the predicted direction, and the missing units are exactly where it said they would be, which is the cheap end of the shelf.

What the theory got wrong is the assumption that this would be visible in any given manufacturer in any given quarter. It is not, because firms respond. A company facing a ceiling will take price in one year and give it back in the next, and its reported volume will swing accordingly while nothing about the underlying constraint has changed at all.

What the theory did not anticipate at all is the shrinkflation channel, which is a third way the ceiling gets paid and the only one that is invisible in both the price line and the volume line.

What would prove this wrong

Three things, and they are all in public quarterly data.

If national brand units turn positive for two consecutive halves while dollars grow faster still, that is a genuine volume recovery and the capped-demand claim is in trouble. If private label unit share falls back below 22 percent, the ratchet was cyclical after all and the 94 percent figure was people describing an intention they did not keep. And if sheet counts start rising again while shelf prices hold, then the ceiling has slackened and the companies have room they did not have.

None of those is present today. All three are checkable in ninety days, which is the point of writing the prediction down.

Two cautions about what is above. The 26 percent figure is arithmetic on two separately reported changes whose periods do not line up exactly, so treat it as the scale of the effect rather than a measured quantity. And the 94 percent is survey evidence, which records what people say they will do. The share data is the stronger claim, because it records what they did.
