The Tightening Nobody Voted On

The Federal Open Market Committee meets on 8 December, and the market gives an increase better than four to one odds. The increase has in a sense already happened. Two hundred basis points of it arrived without a meeting, a statement, or a vote, and almost nobody is counting it.

A reading against the treatise and the Credit Counterfeit Index, four weeks before the December decision. Three claims: the policy rate is not where the tightening is, the inflation that remains traces to a strait and a tariff schedule that no overnight rate reaches, and both branches of the live diplomatic question argue against moving in December. The recommendation is a hold, not a cut. Conditional, falsifiable, not investment advice.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics published its October inflation report on Tuesday. Most of it has a hole in it. There is no twelve month figure for all items. None for core, none for food, none for shelter. The government did not collect prices in October of last year, because it was shut down.

One twelve month number survived the gap.

Gasoline. Up 37.4 percent.

That is the inflation problem, stated by the only part of the index that could still speak.

The dial they are arguing about

On 8 December the committee meets again. Futures put the odds of an increase above 80 percent. Christopher Waller has floated half a point rather than a quarter.

The case looks obvious on its face.

The target range sits at 3.75 to 4 percent. Core PCE inflation ran 3.1 percent in September. Subtract one from the other and the policy rate costs a borrower about three quarters of a percent in real terms. That is not restrictive. It is barely positive.

Growth is not asking for help either. Third quarter output rose at a 3.4 percent annual rate. October payrolls came in at 165,000 against a forecast near 65,000, and the two prior months were revised up by 84,000. Unemployment fell to 4.1 percent.

Core prices have risen 0.3 percent in each of the last three months. Annualize that and you get 3.7.

Two governors already voted to go. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissented against the hold on 28 October, both preferring a quarter point increase. The committee stayed put by ten to two.

So why wait.

Somebody already went

The Treasury publishes two yield curves every afternoon. One is nominal. The other is real, built from inflation protected securities, and it reports what a lender earns after inflation is taken out.

On 10 November the ten year real yield was 2.78 percent.

The real policy rate is 0.78. The real ten year is 2.78. The gap is two hundred basis points, almost to the decimal.

This matters because of a mechanism this site has argued before. Nobody borrows overnight. A household signs a thirty year mortgage. A firm issues a ten year bond. The rate that reaches a real decision is the long one. In real terms it sits two full points higher. The committee does not set it.

Eight quarter point hikes sit in that gap. The long end simply never held a meeting.

The committee is debating whether to tighten. The tightening already happened, two hundred basis points of it, and no member of the committee cast a ballot for it.

It is not only the bond market.

The October Senior Loan Officer survey published on 2 November. Standards for large and middle market firms were basically unchanged. Standards for small firms tightened. Standards on commercial real estate tightened in all three categories. Demand fell for every category of both.

Below investment grade it bites harder. The broad high yield index trades near 334 basis points, which is calm by any historical standard. The CCC tier trades near 1,311. It peaked at 1,356 on 23 October, the widest reading since that index began in 2023.

Credit is already being rationed. It is being rationed at the bottom, which is where rationing always starts and where a policy rate is the clumsiest available instrument.

The day the hot print landed

Now the question of whether anybody doubts the Fed.

October consumer prices came in hotter than forecast on 10 November. Headline rose 0.4 percent against the 0.3 expected, core 0.3 against 0.2. Here is what the ten year did with that.

The yield rose ten basis points. Every one of them, and two more, came out of the real yield.

Which means the inflation expectation inside the ten year fell.

Read that again. The market saw a hot inflation report and marked down its forecast for inflation. The number was 2.35 percent in mid October. It is 2.20 now, against a target of two.

A hike bought to defend credibility would be buying something the Fed already owns.

What no rate reaches

So return the question to the gauge, which was built to ask exactly this.

The Credit Counterfeit Index reads minus 1.36 for the fourth quarter. The monetary bind sits at minus 0.01, which is nothing at all. The real bind sits at 1.35, and it is the only term in the series that has moved in a year.

The real bind is a channel of water. IMF PortWatch counted three transits of the Strait of Hormuz on 8 November, against a pre crisis baseline near 85 a day. Thirty seven vessels were hit in October, the most in any month of the war.

Then there is the other half of the price level, and it is not the Fed's either.

The Supreme Court struck down the emergency powers tariffs six to three in February. The schedule was rebuilt within months under Section 232 and Section 301. The effective rate collected on imports runs near 7 percent and the statutory rate near 11.

A tariff is a price written into law. A closed strait is a quantity. Neither one takes instruction from an overnight rate.

Whichever way it breaks

Brent crude sold for about $117 in late October. On Wednesday it traded near $92.

Nothing physical changed in between.

Qatar's prime minister carried a proposal into the Oval Office on 9 November. Iran stops attacking Gulf shipping. Washington lifts the blockade and releases eight billion dollars of Iranian money held in Doha. Iran has accepted the framework. The United States has not. Marco Rubio called the terms outrageous and ridiculous, and JD Vance is reported to favor pursuing them anyway.

So a twenty one percent move in the world's oil benchmark is riding on a document on a desk.

That is the thing the December meeting is really deciding about, and it has two branches.

IfThenAnd a December hike
The strait reopens Brent falls further from $92. Headline inflation drops through the first quarter with no help from policy. lands on a labor market where the average spell of unemployment already runs 27.1 weeks, the longest since September 2021, and on a CCC tier near its widest since 2023.
The talks collapse Brent returns toward $117 or goes through it. The real bind rises and the gauge falls further below zero. tightens into a physical shortage. No policy rate has ever produced a barrel of oil or opened a shipping channel.

Neither branch wants the hike.

And there is a calendar fact that settles the timing. The committee votes on 9 December. The November inflation report publishes on 10 December, the following morning.

The one number that would separate a core inflation problem from an oil pass through arrives one day too late to be used. By the 26 and 27 January meeting there will be two more core prints in hand, and the diplomatic branch will very likely have resolved.

A central bank can be wrong about the economy. It should try much harder not to be wrong about which question it is answering.

Seven weeks of waiting is cheap when expectations sit at 2.20 and the long end is already two points restrictive. Seven weeks of waiting is expensive only if core inflation is running on its own, independent of the strait. Which is the case worth stating properly, and it comes below.

The cut, since it was asked for

In September the President said the rate should be one percent, or less.

Put it there. Against core PCE of 3.1, the real policy rate becomes roughly minus two. Nothing about the strait changes. Nothing about the tariff schedule changes. What changes is the thing the 10 November print showed was still intact. It is also the hardest item here to buy back.

It is worth saying why the request exists at all.

Net interest on the federal debt reached 1.05 trillion dollars in fiscal 2026, on a stock of 40.2 trillion. The deficit came in at 1.99 trillion, twelve percent wider than the year before, run at 4.1 percent unemployment. That is the largest single reason an administration wants a lower policy rate. It is also the worst available reason to set one.

In fairness, the public pressure has eased. On 28 October the Treasury Secretary said further increases would not necessarily be a policy mistake, and declined to lean on the committee ahead of December. The loudest voice for moving now belongs to a governor the President appointed.

Where the bias goes instead

A hold is not a shrug, and December happens to be the right meeting to prove that.

The 8 and 9 December meeting carries a Summary of Economic Projections. The committee publishes where it expects the rate to sit through 2028. It can move those dots without moving the rate.

That is the instrument for the actual message. We are not cutting. We will move if core does not break on its own.

The case for hiking, stated fairly

It is a serious case, and it rests on one sentence. Core excludes energy.

If the strait were the whole story, core should have softened by now. It has not. Three consecutive months at 0.3 percent is a core run rate near 3.7, and a closed waterway does not explain shelter at 0.3.

Then add the rest of it. Nominal output is growing above 7 percent. M2 is up 5.7 percent over the year. The deficit is widening at full employment, and the real policy rate is under one percent against any measure of inflation you choose.

If that describes a committee behind the curve, every argument above is wrong. The quarter point should have come in October.

The honest version of this dispatch is not that the hike is wrong. It is that the hike is early, and that the thing which would settle it publishes the morning after the vote.

What would prove this wrong

Four tests, in order of how fast they resolve.

A fourth consecutive 0.3 percent core print on 10 December, with Brent still under $95. That separates core persistence from oil pass through, and it means the hold was a mistake.

The ten year inflation expectation back above 2.50. That is credibility slipping, and it would make the hike correct regardless of the strait. The number is published daily and needs no interpretation.

The strait reopening while core stays at 0.3. Then energy was never the explanation, and January should move.

And the test in the other direction. Payroll growth under 50,000, or unemployment back to 4.3 percent while the average spell of unemployment keeps lengthening. Then even the hold is too tight, and the monetary bind is about to start rising for real.

Two tests from the September reading are worth scoring in public while we are here, because neither has triggered. That dispatch named a rotation test. The diagnosis turns monetary when two things happen at once. The bind rises above zero, and high yield spreads widen past 400 basis points. The bind is minus 0.01. Spreads are at 334. It also said the real bind would recede on its own if Brent fell under $80 with open transit. Brent is at $92 and the transit count is three a day. Half of one test, which counts for nothing.
