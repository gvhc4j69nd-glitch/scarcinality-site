Two Cents on the Dollar

Ask who profits from medical bankruptcy and the honest answer is almost nobody, which is worse than the alternative. A transfer has a recipient you can tax or regulate. This is closer to a fire. Here is what the debt is actually worth, what the counting does and does not show, and where the cost lands instead.

A companion to The Write-Off and the Wage, which handled the tax question and the 62-versus-4 fight. This piece takes the next ones: the annual series, the amounts, who collects, and what follows. Three claims: the yearly count does not exist and cannot be constructed, the debt is nearly worthless as an asset, and the measurable damage is in housing. Conditional and falsifiable.

A Federal Trade Commission analysis of the debt-buying market found that buyers paid, on average, under two cents on the dollar for medical debt. Credit card debt went for about five.

A former hospital billing executive gave the reason without any varnish. The debt is cheap because it is not collectable. The people do not have the money.

Hold that. It answers the question this dispatch was asked, and it answers it the wrong way round.

First, the number that does not exist

The request was for medical bankruptcies by year, and for the medical balances owed, going back to 1975.

Neither series exists.

Not withheld. Not hard to find. Never created.

Here is why. A bankruptcy petition asks what you owe and to whom. It does not ask why. No federal form has a box marked medical. So the courts publish a clean annual count of filings, and no count at all of causes.

The filings themselves are well measured, and have been since 1980.

Every one of those numbers is solid. None of them tells you how many were medical, and no amount of care with the filings will produce that.

The 1975 start is impossible for a second reason. The modern code dates from 1978, and nobody attempted to estimate medical causes until the early 2000s.

What the estimates actually give you

Two studies dominate, and the site has already walked through why they disagree.

Himmelstein and colleagues surveyed people who had filed and put the medical share near 62 percent. Dobkin, Finkelstein, Kluender and Notowidigdo matched hospital records to credit reports and found hospitalization caused about 4 percent of filings among nonelderly adults.

Both are peer-reviewed. Both are about the same country. Apply them to last year. Look at what you get.

Twenty-two thousand households, or three hundred and forty thousand. The policy you would write is completely different at each end. The United States has never bothered to find out which end it is at.

The amounts, and what each one measures

The balances are better measured than the filings, though not by much. And the three headline figures are not three estimates of one thing.

About 20 million adults carry some. Roughly 3 million owe more than $10,000.

So who profits

Now back to the two cents, and to the specific suspects in the question.

SuspectWhat they actually get
The hospitalNothing from the bankruptcy. The bill was already written off before it was sold, at under two cents on the dollar. The tax treatment is smaller and stranger than reputed, and for a cash-basis practice it is literally nothing.
The debt buyerA thin margin on a near-worthless asset. Buying at two cents and collecting four is a double, but it is a double on almost nothing, from people with nothing.
The lawyersThe only party reliably paid. A Chapter 7 runs about $1,000 to $3,000 in attorney fees plus a $338 court fee, and the fee is collected in cash before filing, because the discharge would otherwise wipe it out.
The accountantsNothing meaningful. There is no accounting product being sold here.
The debtorDischarge, and a credit record that follows them for up to ten years.

Look at what that adds up to.

A household owes, say, $20,000. The hospital sells the paper for about $400. The household pays $2,000 to be rid of it. The collector recovers a few hundred, maybe.

Nobody ends up richer. Not one party.

Twenty thousand dollars of claim becomes a few hundred dollars of recovery, two thousand dollars of professional fees, and a decade of damaged credit.

This is not a transfer from the sick to somebody wealthier. It is a bonfire. Which is harder to fix, because a transfer has a recipient you can tax, and a fire has only ash.

The one genuine profit center in the picture is not bankruptcy at all. It is the collection activity that happens to people who never file: the lawsuits, the garnishments, the years of payment plans. Those extract real money. Bankruptcy is what happens when that extraction has already failed.

Where the cost actually lands

If nobody gains, the money has to go somewhere. It goes into housing.

A Johns Hopkins study published in January used a national panel of 1,515 adults surveyed from 2023 to 2025. Among adults with medical debt, 23.5 percent experienced housing instability in the following year. Among those without, it was 5.8 percent.

That is trouble paying rent or a mortgage, eviction, and foreclosure. Four times the rate.

Renters are about 40 percent of adults with medical debt. That is well above their share of the population. It is what you would expect if the bills reach the people with the least cushion.

At the far end, a University of Washington study looked at 60 adults in King County. Those whose bills had gone to collections had been homeless an average of 22 months longer. That is sixty people and a master's thesis, so it is a thread to pull rather than a finding to lean on.

The direction is consistent across the evidence even where the size is not. Medical debt does not stay in healthcare. It moves into the housing system, and then into the emergency systems that catch people who lose housing. Both are funded by somebody else.

And the measurement just got worse

One more thing happened that the usual coverage missed.

A federal rule would have removed about $49 billion of medical debt from the credit reports of roughly 15 million Americans. A court vacated it on 11 July 2025, after the agency that wrote it joined the motion to strike it down.

Set aside whether the rule was good policy. Notice what it would have done to the record.

The $88 billion visible on credit reports is one of only two national handles anybody has on this debt. Removing $49 billion of it would have cut that handle roughly in half. For the best of reasons, and leaving the country even less able to see what it is arguing about.

The scarcinality reading

The framework asks whether a shortage is real or manufactured. Here it asks something narrower and harder.

Nothing is scarce in this story. The care was delivered. The hospital stands, the staff were paid, the drugs were administered. Every real resource involved was supplied and consumed before the bankruptcy existed.

What is left afterward is a claim, and the claim turns out to be worth two cents.

That is the purest counterfeit shortage on this site. A household is destroyed over an obligation the market prices at nearly zero. The destruction is real even though the thing causing it is not.

And it joins the pattern this site keeps finding. Activity nobody records. Instruments too impaired to read. A quantity argued over for twenty years. It could be settled by one more question on a form millions already fill out under oath.

When the other reading is right

The strongest case against all of this deserves stating.

Suppose the Dobkin estimate is closer to true. Then medical bankruptcy is a small phenomenon that has absorbed an enormous share of the health policy argument. The attention would be better spent on a far larger group: the people who carry medical debt for years and never file at all. That group does not show up in bankruptcy statistics by definition.

On that reading the fixation on bankruptcy is itself a measurement error. It counts the visible endpoint because it is visible, and misses the 20 million people upstream.

This piece thinks that argument is strong. It is also an argument for measuring more, not less.

What would prove this wrong

Three tests.

The courts may one day add a cause field and the medical share may land near 4 percent. Then the framing here overstates the scale, though not the measurement gap.

Medical debt may start selling at prices comparable to credit card paper. That would mean it is more collectable than the FTC analysis found, and the bonfire argument weakens considerably.

And the Johns Hopkins housing association may fail to replicate in a larger panel. Then the downstream cost claim loses its best evidence and falls back on a sixty-person study. That would not be enough.

Cautions. The filing counts are calendar-year figures from the courts and the American Bankruptcy Institute. The years 2012 through 2017 were not reliably available on a consistent basis. The table shows selected years rather than a continuous series. The two-cent figure is an FTC analysis of the debt-buying market reported secondhand, and prices vary by portfolio age and quality. The $220 billion is a survey estimate the authors describe as a floor. The housing study is an association in panel data, not a causal estimate, and the homelessness study is 60 people. And the central claim here is a negative one, which is the hardest kind to prove. A series may exist somewhere that this research did not surface. Finding it would be a correction worth making.
